# Changelog

## Unreleased (working tree)

### Quality of life
- **Tim explains defending, once, before you meet the Defense tab.** Defending is the one part of Zombie Farm the player never actually plays — the farm holds the line while they are somewhere else — so the Invasions panel was the first screen in the game with rules nobody had stated: that you pick your strongest of each *kind* rather than marching out an army, and that a defense costs nothing, because no zombie is ever lost holding the place. Both are now said out loud the first time the panel opens, in Tim Buckwheat's voice and on the same portrait and speech bubble the tutorial uses. It is a one-shot, dismissed by a button and remembered per device, and it layers over the panel rather than blocking it — the tabs stay usable behind him.
- **Invasions open at level 7, the Black Market at level 10, and both say so.** Neither is a surface a brand-new farm can do anything with: invading needs an army worth sending and a defense worth holding, and trading needs a roster worth posting and brains worth spending. Both were already unusable that early in practice — friend invasions have always had a level floor on both sides of the fight, and a level-2 farm browsing the market has nothing to offer and nothing to spend. They are now plainly shut instead of quietly useless. The Social hub still *lists* both, greyed, with "Unlocks at level N" where the description usually sits: that entry is the only place a player learns the feature is coming and what reaching it costs, so hiding it would have been worse than locking it. The market's floor is held by the server too, on the two calls that actually move something (posting and trading), so it is a rule rather than a hidden button — and below it, selling a zombie skips straight to the gold sale instead of offering a Black Market post the server would refuse.
- **You can delete your farm, from the Account menu, and it is genuinely gone.** Online it removes the account row and every row that references it — fifty-nine columns across fifty-three tables — which frees the Google id along with it, so signing in again does not resurrect anything: it falls through the ordinary create path and starts a brand-new farm with a new id and a new friend code. Local Farm's button does the same to the save and its backup, and the reload lands on the ordinary new-game path. Either way "start again" needed no separate reset to implement and keep faithful; it is what a farm the game has never seen already does. It asks twice, and the two dialogs are deliberately not the same dialog written out twice: the first states exactly what is destroyed and that there is no backup and no recovery, the second asks for a differently-worded confirmation ("Yes, delete my account") in a button that is not where the first one's was, so the second tap cannot land on muscle memory from the first. Neither is markPrimary, so a stray Enter confirms neither, and there is no "don't ask again". Three things it refuses to do: it will not delete while a command batch or raid settlement is still in flight; it will not delete while the account has an open Black Market post or a fulfilled trade whose zombie has not been claimed, because that would cascade another player's owed zombie away with it (the player is told to finish up first); and it nulls rather than deletes the one column that records who *filled* somebody else's order, since that row is their completed trade, not this account's. The list of what to clear is written out in source rather than read from SQLite at run time, because D1 refuses `pragma_foreign_key_list` outright — so a test loads the real `schema.sql`, asks SQLite which columns reference accounts, and requires the answer to match exactly, which turns a future migration into a failing test rather than into rows left behind by a player who asked to be forgotten.
- **Usernames are filtered.** The name a player chooses is the only thing about them other players see, and it does not stay among friends — every Black Market listing carries its creator's name to everyone browsing. The existing rule was shape-only, and because it is an allowlist it was already turning away the structural tricks (zero-width characters, right-to-left overrides, stacked combining marks, emoji); what it let through was ordinary evasion. So a name is now folded before it is judged — homoglyphs from other alphabets mapped onto their Latin twins, fullwidth forms normalised, leetspeak resolved, padding stripped — and then read at two levels, because one rule cannot both catch "s.h.i.t" and spare Scunthorpe. Slurs are matched anywhere in the folded name — but only the long ones, because the short ones are not the safe case they look like: `paki`, `nazi`, `negro`, `chink`, `kike` and `rapist` sit inside Pakistan, Nazir, Negroponte, Chinkara, Kikelomo and therapist, and an anywhere-match on them refused a player's own nationality or given name as a racial slur. Those are matched as WORDS instead, so they still catch the name used as that word and still catch every evasion of it, and they still refuse as a slur rather than as ordinary profanity. Ordinary profanity is matched on word boundaries, and only matched anywhere when the name was padded out — because somebody typing a word one letter at a time is evading, not naming a mushroom. A third list of the words that genuinely do live inside real names is never matched anywhere at all, which is what lets Bass99, Cumbria, Penistone, Cockburn and Analysis through. Staff and maintainer names are judged on the whole name, so "Admin" is refused and "Administrator of Turnips" is not. A refused name now says which kind of problem it was rather than repeating the length rule — which matters most at the sign-in gate, where the name picker is the only way through and “check your connection” is advice that can never work, since the same name is refused every time. Impersonation gets its own line, because that player wrote nothing offensive and “reserved” is the only word that explains why an ordinary name bounced. The wording lives in one place both surfaces render through, so the gate and Settings cannot drift apart. The word lists are data — extending one is a single line, and the tests pin the behaviour rather than the contents so growing a list cannot break them.
- **A Privacy page in the Farmer's Guide.** The game now says, in plain language and where a player will actually look, what it knows about them — and the honest answer is startlingly little, which is worth saying out loud rather than leaving buried in a source comment. A Local Farm contacts nothing at all. Signing in with Google hands the game a token carrying an anonymous id, an email address and a display name; it reads the id and throws the other two away, so no email is ever stored, sent, or shown. What sits on the server is that id, the name the player chose, their friend code, when they last played, and the farm itself — no email, no IP addresses, no location, no real names. The page is equally plain about the parts a player might not expect: the name they choose is visible to every farmer browsing the Black Market, not just to friends, so it says so and tells them to choose accordingly. It also covers what a visiting friend can see (a look-only copy with currencies zeroed and quests, invasions, storage and the friend list not sent at all), that there is no advertising, analytics or third-party tracking of any kind — enforced, not merely promised, by a browser security policy that permits the game to reach only Google's sign-in and its own server — that crash diagnostics stay on the device unless the player pastes them somewhere themselves, and how to export a farm or have an account deleted outright. Each claim is a claim about code, and `farmersGuide.test.ts` now pins them, so a change that breaks one fails a test rather than quietly turning the page into a lie. **The deletion promise is the one the page could most easily have been lying about, so it is code now rather than a promise:** the Account menu deletes the farm outright — see the deletion entry above.
- **A zombie collected from the Zombie Pot climbs out of the Pot.** It used to appear wherever the farmer happened to be standing when the button was pressed, which on a big farm meant a child brewed in a Pot in the far corner materialising at the player's feet — it reads as the farmer having grown it. Collection now places it on the open ground beside the Pot it came out of (the Pot's own tiles are built on, so the search steps out to the nearest tile a zombie can stand on), and the name that pops up appears there too. If the Pot is gone by the time the collection lands — sold, or dropped by an object reconcile — it falls back to the farmer as before, which is still better than the field's top corner. A child sent straight to the Mausoleum is unaffected: it never stood anywhere.
- **A Statistics panel, in the Account menu.** Opened from the nameplate beside the farm's name: level and total XP, how long the count has been running, crops harvested with the favourite crop named and counted, crops planted, plots plowed, trees picked, gold and brains both in hand and lifetime earned/spent, zombies grown / combined / sold / lost with the two Almanacs' collection totals, and invasions won, lost and the win rate. Almost none of it is recoverable from a save after the fact — the farm holds the crops standing in it, not the ten thousand it has harvested — so it is a **kept tally**, counted where each thing happens and carried in the save (and, online, in the presentation blob beside the Almanac, since there is no server table it could be rebuilt from). Gold and brains are the exception and are read off the BALANCE rather than hooked onto each path: currency reaches a farm through half a dozen routes, and the balance is the one thing they all agree on — which is also what stops an online harvest being counted once optimistically and again when the server confirms it. Loading a save is not income, so a signed-in account does not book its whole fortune as earnings every session. A farm that predates the tally starts counting from the load, with the one figure that IS derivable — invasions won — seeded from the raid progress it already carries, so a veteran does not open the panel to a claim that they have never won a fight. **The tally follows the account, not the device.** Online it rides the presentation blob, so signing in elsewhere restores it — and because that blob is written wholesale, two things defend it: a write that loses the version CAS folds the winning blob's tally into its own and keeps the higher of each counter (they only climb, so neither side's work is lost) rather than rebasing this device's numbers over another's, and the server carries a stored tally forward past any client too old to send one instead of storing that client's silence as a reset. Neither can raise a counter. There is still a window — presentation writes are debounced, so a tab killed mid-session can lose up to a minute of counting, exactly as it can for zombie names and the Almanac.
- **Optional health-bar and damage numbers in invasions, both off by default** (Settings → Display). On, every health bar prints the HP left over its maximum, and each hit floats its damage off the unit that took it — the player's own losses in red, damage the army deals in pale gold. ZF2 printed neither and the bars alone are the look most players expect, hence the default; this is for anyone who wants to see the arithmetic. Neither touches the fight: both are read off HP the simulation has already decided, so a raid plays out tick-for-tick identically with them on, which is what lets them ride a server-verified deterministic replay at all. Sub-point damage (a `pixelFire` burn removes 5% of max HP a second across twenty ticks) accumulates instead of printing a column of zeroes, hits on the same unit inside a fifth of a second are shown as one figure, and the killing blow is always shown. A unit still standing on a sliver reads 1, never 0. Both switches apply from the next invasion, and with them off nothing extra is built or drawn.

### Invasions
- **The seasonal invasions spread out along the ladder: Valentine's Day at level 6, Tree World at 8, Summer Break at 10.** All three shipped stacked at level 8 (`level` and `reccomendedLevel` alike in the authored data), so crossing one level dumped three new invasions on the player at once and then offered nothing until the Circus at 12. They now arrive one at a time. The override lives in `prep_raids.py` (`LEVEL_OVERRIDES`), moves `unlockLevel` and `recommendedLevel` together, and everything priced off a raid's rung re-fits on its own: the boss-throw yardstick leaves Valentine's throws exactly where they were, because the reference healer sits on its level-8 con floor all the way down (250 HP at rung 6 and rung 8 alike), and eases Summer Break's by about 7% (15/0/44/44 → 14/0/41/41) because a level-10 player's best healer really is slightly frailer than a level-8 player's — the yardstick following the healer, as authored. The brain-drop ramp nudges the same directions with `recommendedLevel`. The repeat-XP trickle keeps its hand-set 12s for all three, which still read as a rising ladder around the new rungs (10 → 12/12/12 → 14), and Tree World's two Goffy quests already asked level 8 and still match; no quest references the other two invasions. In-flight online fights are untouched — the server pins each fight's config at launch.
- **A revived zombie re-enters at the back of the line — except Headless, which pushes to the absolute front.** Arrival order is the formation order, and resurrection is a fresh arrival after the living line has closed over the corpse. Ordinary zombies therefore receive a new tail rank, just like a zombie returning from knockback. Headless keeps its defining exception on every arrival path: an initial deployment, a return from knockback, or a resurrection assigns it a new rank ahead of every deployed zombie, including other Headless zombies already fighting.
- **A revived zombie stops for the abductee again (Zombies vs Aliens).** The saucer's beamed-down human is a mid-lane roadblock, and a zombie already *past* it when it landed carries a latch that says so, which is what stops a knocked-back zombie turning round to fight something that appeared behind it. A revive comes back at the charge slot — plainly behind the roadblock — but kept the latch anyway, so it walked straight through the abductee it had marched past in its first life and never traded a blow with it. On the one raid whose boss re-summons the moment the last abductee dies, that made the roadblock clearable only by zombies that had never died. It re-enters the lane like any other zombie now, and re-earns the latch by marching past.
- **The saucer shoots at whatever is on the lane (Zombies vs Aliens).** Its laser used to pick only from zombies actually *toe-to-toe with the wave*, which is what the binary does — and it had two costs. A Garden zombie holds a fixed spot and closes on nothing, so it is never in that set: an army of nothing but healers, or one whose last ordinary body had died, handed the saucer an empty target list on every cycle, and the invasion ran out its four-minute clock with neither side able to touch the other. Less dramatically, that list also went empty for seconds at a stretch in ordinary fights — between waves, and while the front rank walked across — so the saucer would just stop firing for no reason a player could see. It now draws from **every deployed zombie**, so it is shooting whenever you have anything out there, down to a single Garden on its own. What it does with that list is unchanged and is the part that matters: it still picks **one target at random**, so a healer takes its share of the fire and not the whole barrage — the old bug this rule originally replaced aimed the bolt like a *throw*, at the rear-most zombie, which put every single shot on the support station. Measured: on the balance stick the raid moves 0.98 → 0.99 ordinary and 2.12 → 2.21 elite with no win or loss flipping, and the late-invasion elite ramp still climbs in order; a lone healer standing behind an eight-strong army now dies to the laser during the fight instead of surviving it.
- Raid ruleset bumped 44 → 45 for the three above.

### Balance
- **Boss projectiles scale with the fight they are thrown in.** A boss's throw damage is a flat number authored in the raid data and applied verbatim to a zombie whose hit points are `con x 100`, so it never tracked the rung the raid sits on. Measured over whole invasions, the throws took **0%** off a lone Garden healer on the Pirates and **1%** on the Lawyers — the healer being the exact unit a boss lobs at, since the throw goes over the front line and lands on the support station at the back. The Ninjas managed 37%, the Robots 47%. The projectile had stopped being a mechanic. Throw damage is now re-based onto the raid's own level, against one yardstick: **fifteen seconds of a boss holding its throws on one target kills an unmutated, level-appropriate healer that no second healer is supporting.** "Level-appropriate" is the healer a player on that rung would actually own — the most recently *purchasable* Garden zombie, gold market only (Garden Zombie → ZomBotanist → Flower Zombie → Zombee), level-scaled exactly as a real one would be; the brain-gated Cupids are excluded, since at con 15 against the ladder's top rung of 5.5 they would make the reference "the best healer obtainable" rather than "the healer this level has reached". Every boss keeps its authored shape: the light/medium/heavy split and each piece of debris's frequency are untouched (one factor scales the whole table), the raid's own throw cadence is untouched, and the three authored duds — the Beach fishbone, the Tree apple, the Valentine spoon — stay at zero, because those are the boss missing. The floor never *lowers* an authored value, so the alien saucer's 200-damage laser bolt and Zedzox's flat 100s — the hardest-hitting authored throw in the game — stand exactly as the binary has them.

  **Old McDonnell's is the one invasion fitted to a DPS band rather than to that window, and the figures above are the top of his ladder.** Fitting to a fixed time-to-kill makes throw damage per second the same whatever the cadence is, which is right everywhere except here: his is the only raid whose cadence carries meaning. His stages speed up as the ladder climbs — one throw every 2 seconds, then 1.5, then 1 — and the first two clears are eased on top of that, at half and two-thirds speed, in step with the army ramp that lets a brand-new player launch the tutorial invasion with a single zombie. Fitted flat, all nine of those combinations came out at exactly the same 16.7 damage a second: the easing bought nothing but rarer, heavier hits, and the ladder's own escalation was flattened out of existence. So his throws are fitted to a band instead — **12 damage a second at the slowest fight the game can present, 20 at the fastest**, and every combination in between in proportion to its pace. The opening armed stage on a first clear now throws at four-fifths the pressure the same stage deals once it has been mastered, and the top of the ladder hits two-thirds harder again than its foot.

  The Lawyers and the Pirates move furthest because they move off a floor rather than off the window: both throw far too slowly (one lob every five and eight seconds) to spend fifteen seconds' worth of damage across a sane number of hits, so they sit on the "no fewer than three landed heavies" clamp. Measured after, in the real simulator with a minimum-size army carrying one healer and no second one: **nine of the ten throwing invasions take that healer from full to dead.** The Robots reach 99%, and the reason is not the damage — the BrainBot spends a quarter of its action budget on a six-second telekinesis cast and then leaves its perch to fight, and a boss off its perch throws nothing at all, so it lands about twelve throws in a two-minute fight where its authored cadence implies sixty. The Aliens are untouched because their boss has no throw table at all — its projectile is the laser, and the laser deliberately burns whoever is toe-to-toe with the wave rather than the healers massed at the back. Old McDonnell's is still eased for a new player by the thing that already eased it: throwing is off entirely on its opening stage, and the first two clears run its cadence at half and two-thirds speed.
- **Every elite invasion's projectile is re-fitted, because a Brain Ticket was charging the rebalance above twice.** `throwDamage` in an elite profile multiplies whatever the ordinary fight throws, and until now that was the authored chip value — so those multipliers had never been fitted against anything that mattered, and it showed: measured as damage a second against the same reference healer, elite projectiles ranged from 11 (the Lawyers, which needed twenty-two seconds to kill it) to 259 (the Circus, which needed eight tenths of one). Left alone on top of the rebalance the two multiplied together, and elite Circus reached **343 a second — a healer deleted in six tenths of a second, from off screen, before the army had finished walking out**. Each profile's throw is now a step over the *rebalanced* throw, in a **2x to 4x band**, and the band's order is the old table's own: the raids whose ordinary throws the rebalance lifted hardest (Pirates 4.42x, Lawyers 3.65x) keep the smallest step, because their elite throw is now a step on a far heavier base — elite Pirates still throw an anchor for 243 against elite Summer Break's 56. How OFTEN a boss throws is untouched, so every boss keeps its character: the Circus ringmaster still juggles at three times the rate. The Video Games are the control and came through unchanged at 2.22x, which is right — Zedzox's authored throws already cleared the floor, so his ordinary fight never moved. **The Circus also takes its difficulty back in strength and hit points** (x2 on both multipliers' distance from 1): it is the only invasion whose boss has no action *but* the throw, so its projectile was carrying a real share of the elite fight rather than merely looking absurd — cutting it alone took the raid off the rung it is fitted to (the Robots, normally), and the wave carries that weight now instead. Its attack speed is deliberately not part of the move. No elite fight lands within reach of the four-minute replay cap, and the elite ladder still climbs in the same order it did.
- **Zombies vs Video Games pays 6,300 base, up from 5,000.** It is the last invasion unlocked and the hardest fight on the ladder, and at the old figure it paid barely more per run than the Aliens five levels below it. A flawless run now tops out at 7,500 with the bonus. Priced in three places that have to agree — `tools/prep_raids.py` WIKI_GOLD (which regenerates raids.json), and the server's own `raidCatalog.ts`, since the Worker prices the reward rather than trusting the client.
- **New quest: "Game Over, Zedzox" — win Zombies vs Video Games three times, for 6,000 XP.** Every other invasion already had a "beat this raid 3x" quest; the ladder ran McDonnell → Lawyers → Pirates → Ninjas → Robots → Aliens and then stopped, leaving the game's last raid without one. Gated at 43 to match its unlock. The 6,000 is not arbitrary: the catalog's dominance invariant sets a hard floor of 5,600 here (no quest may pay less than one that unlocks no later and asks no more of you — "Untouchable" at level 40 pays that for a single action), and 6,000 continues the ladder's own ramp of 200 → 400 → 800 → 2,000 → 3,200 → 4,000.

- **Brain Ticket invasions are a ramp now, not a band.** Falling out of the `turnZombie` fix below. The five late invasions used to end in a pile-up: the Robots, the Aliens and the Video Games all sat in one shared top tier, and the Video Games' own elite profile was kept deliberately tiny — a Brain Ticket on the hardest raid in the game bought you the brains and barely changed the fight. Both were worked around the same thing: raid 9's ordinary difficulty measured 2.11–2.72 on the balance stick, so there was no headroom left above it. That number was the instant kill, not the design. With the conversion in place the ordinary fight measures 1.33, and the five re-fit into a smooth climb in the order you unlock them — Pirates **1.50**, Ninjas **1.67**, Robots **1.82**, Aliens **2.04**, Video Games **2.21** — in even steps of about 0.15–0.2. The last invasion you unlock is now also the hardest elite fight in the game, which it never actually was. Every profile keeps its shape (one scale factor per raid, applied to each multiplier's distance from 1.0), so no invasion plays differently — the Pirates still one-shot you, the Ninjas still hide behind a wall — only where each one sits. The Video Games' Brain Ticket step goes 1.27× → 1.66×, in line with the rest of the table, and the balance suite's "three hardest share one band" guardrail is replaced by one that asserts the ramp climbs and stays smooth.
- **Protect now shields every body type — including the Headless who carry it.** The aura's damage reduction was written `group === "Headless" ? 0 : carriers × 20%` at both places that compute it (the roster build and the per-tick aura refresh), so the game's tank group was the only one that could not be shielded — and Protect is a *Headless* ability, so it excluded exactly the bodies that bring it. A Bombie holds the highest hit points in the roster and still took every blow raw. Nothing in the recovered notes justifies the exclusion, but it was encoding something real, clumsily: a carrier does not shield **itself**, only the rest of the line. That rule is kept — a lone Protect zombie still gets nothing from its own aura — while a real Headless line now protects each of its members with every *other* carrier's 20%, up to the same 95% cap, computed in one shared place (`combatStats.protectReduction`) so the tick refresh can never silently undo the build again. Measured where it matters most: a realistic mixed 16-party fields three Headless, so each takes 40% and the Dread Pirate Arrrnold's 5,000-damage slam lands as 3,000 — survivable outright by a master Bombie's 4,084.
- **The Dread Pirate Arrrnold clobbers anything that moves too fast.** His authored slam deals 5,000 against a roster whose best max hit points are 5,513, so constitution buys nothing against him: everything short of the very top dies to one hit, and the one-shot protection latch meant even the tankiest body died on exactly his second swing whatever its build. The fight was stat-proof — no army composition changed how it went, only how long it took. He now mirrors the attack speed of the zombie he is facing, exactly as his Scallywag minion always has (`max(0.5 s, interval² / 0.8)`): a fast front line drives him toward the half-second floor, while a slow one holds him back — a master Bombie's 1.6 s swing pins him to 3.2 s, slower than his authored 2.5 s — with break-even at a 1.414 s zombie, which lands exactly on his authored pace. Headless already takes the front slot by priority, so the counter-play is reachable with the zombies a player would bring anyway: park something slow and heavy in front of him. This is a deliberate divergence, stated as such in the code (`combatStats.PIRATE_BOSS_KEY`): the binary reaches the mirror through the Scallywag class alone, but the raid's own failure text — "Rumors say pirates clobber anything that moves too fast" — only ever came true of the minion, and reading it as the family trait it claims to be gives dexterity a job in the one fight where hit points have none. Measured on 16-strong master armies at levels 21/30/45, with the Protect fix riding along: a mixed party is unchanged at 15/16 survivors, all-Headless goes 11/16 → 16/16, no-Headless 12/16 → 11/16, and no win or loss flips. **Raid ruleset 37 → 38** for the pair — both change transcripts from the first contact, and an invasion in flight at deploy time settles as `stale_ruleset` and pays nothing.

### Saves
- **A farm decorated to the object limit no longer stops saving its layout.** The presentation blob — zombie names, teams, the Almanac, object layout, camera, lifetime counters — is validated wholesale and refused wholesale, and the layout's size bound was written as a literal 512 beside an object cap that is also 512. But the layout carries one thing the object document never does: the free starter shed, which stays presentation-only until it is upgraded. So a player who filled their farm to the cap sent 513 layouts against a bound of 512, and **every** one of those things silently stopped saving from then on — only the most decorated farms in the game could reach it. The bound is derived from the cap now, plus that one shed, and pinned so a literal cannot creep back in.
- **And a refused save says so.** A rejected presentation write never clears by retrying — the same blob goes back every minute and is refused every time — yet nothing recorded it: no log, no diagnostic, no word to the player, who would find out by reloading hours later into a farm that had forgotten its names and its layout. It now warns once per fault, and writes the reason into Settings › Diagnostics so a report can carry it. The ordinary version race is untouched; it was already handled.
- **The save format can be changed without deleting every offline farm.** Every reader compared the stored version against the current one and treated any difference as damage. Nothing had ever migrated, because each field added since launch was optional with a default — which is precisely what made it dangerous: the first person to bump that constant would have turned every Local Farm on every device into "Can't Open Your Local Farm", whose only other button deletes the save *and* its backup. A one-line change sat one click away from wiping every offline player with their bytes perfectly intact. There is now one place that knows what an old save looked like, a save from a newer build is still refused rather than guessed at, and a test fails if the version moves without a way to read what came before it.
- **Importing a save into a full browser no longer reports success.** The write could fail while the import returned true, so the player was told it had worked and then reloaded into the farm they were trying to replace.

### Stability
- **An invasion you walked away from can still be won.** A session got fifteen minutes of wall clock, and a fight settled after that was answered with nothing — no replay, no rewards, whatever the battle had actually done, though your zombies all came home (which is why the report shape was always "no rewards *and* I lost nobody"). But the battle does not run on wall clock. It is driven by the frame ticker, so a backgrounded tab or a locked phone freezes the fight while the deadline keeps counting: eleven sessions across eleven accounts in eight days of play, between 18 minutes and 13 hours late, one apiece — not a defect, just people walking away mid-fight and coming back. The deadline predates deterministic replay and outlived its reason. Nothing about a late transcript is easier to forge than a prompt one: the fight replays byte-for-byte from the configuration pinned when it started, a finish still cannot claim more ticks than wall clock allows, and no replay can exceed four simulated minutes however long the session has been open. So the clock was gating rewards it was not protecting. It now governs only what it can actually govern — when an **abandoned** session lets go of its roster — and a late finish is judged on its merits like any other. Fifteen minutes is kept rather than raised, deliberately: that lock is the real boundary, because while it is held nothing else can have touched those zombies, which is exactly what makes settling late safe. Once it is released — by a reload, a resync, or another invasion — the fight really is over, since those units are free again and may have fought elsewhere since. The in-fight warnings say that now instead of announcing a loss that was not happening: finish it, and don't reload.
- **Every production client now says which bundle it is.** `X-Client-Build` was sent from a `VITE_BUILD_ID` that nothing has ever set — not the deploy workflow, not any env file — so every online player reported `build:"dev"` and the server's logs could not tell an old bundle from a new one. That is the exact question worth answering after shipping a fix for a bug that was never reproduced. There was never a second value to populate, only a second name to stop using: the short commit SHA has been filled from the build all along for the crash report, and the header now sends that same identity as `version+sha`. Pinned so a third name cannot appear.
- **An abandoned request can no longer wedge the command lane for good.** `active_batch_id` is a shared marker: `/commands` sets and clears it inside one transaction, but every raid, epic-boss, gift, Black Market, presentation and save request also sets it, and only that request's own completion clears it. A Worker killed between the two leaves one behind, and nothing sweeps it — D1 has no TTL, which is exactly why the other two readers evaluate the expiry lazily at read time. The batch path was the last one that did not, in **two** places: the guard at the top and the compare-and-swap at the bottom, which independently required the marker to be absent rather than merely expired. So one orphan answered every subsequent batch with a conflict for good — on an account whose lease was live and being renewed by all the *other* routes, which is the player-visible "Gameplay paused — reconnect to continue" on a connection that is fine. Both now accept a marker past its two-minute TTL, matching what the lease-release path was already fixed to do; relaxing only one of them would have swapped a permanent `batch_in_progress` for a permanent `state_conflict`, which is the same dead farm.
- **A raid result the server refuses no longer stops the game from loading.** A completed invasion whose settlement POST fails is kept on the device and resent by the next bootstrap — and that resend runs inside *every* bootstrap: boot, the recovery backoff, the conflict reload, the mobile writer re-claim, every resync. Its errors propagated, so a transcript the server will never accept (a roster partition it rejects, or an outcome shape a newer bundle validates differently) failed the whole bootstrap — and because the throw jumped over the line that discards the transcript, the same one was waiting to do it again. Nothing else settles that session either, so the account could not load at all until the server's own fifteen-minute session expiry cleared the fight: a quarter of an hour of "reconnecting" over a raid the player had already finished. A finish that fails on the wire is still kept and still retried; one that is refused outright is dropped, because it will be refused for as long as it exists and keeping it only spreads that one raid's loss across everything else.
- **A completed quest is retired from the rail before anything else can go wrong.** The offline sweep that closes quests already at their target repeats until a pass finds nothing new, and its termination rests entirely on completion removing the id from the active list — but the completion routine bailed out early on an id it had already seen, *before* that removal. No path reaches that state today (every writer to the active list checks the completed set first), so this is not a fix for a live bug; it is a one-line change that makes the failure impossible by construction rather than by audit, because the failure is a hard freeze — no error, no frame, just a dead page.

### Debugging

- **A bug report now says what the game was doing, not only what crashed.** The two reports that cost the most this beta — the Epic Boss green screen and settings not surviving a restart — both arrived saying `errors: none recorded`, and both were telling the truth: nothing threw. The green screen was a *stall*, three seconds inside a `catch` that returns `null` and behaves exactly as written, which no exception-based capture could ever have seen. So the diagnostics report carries a short **activity trail** as well: the last forty steps, each with the **gap since the previous one**, which is the column that names a stall — `battle:launch` → `battle:ready +4110ms` would have handed over a bug that instead took an hour of forensics. It survives a reload on purpose, because the failure it was built for hides the whole HUD, so the player cannot copy a report until they have reloaded to escape, and an in-memory trail would be gone by then. This pass instruments the two seams that already cost real time: **battle load** (launch → ready/failed → left, on invasions and Epic Bosses alike, with the fight named and the army size) and **unloadable textures**.
- **The save and the command queue are on the trail too.** Saves record their outcome and SIZE (`save:local 3kB`, `save:online 12kB v41`), a refused write records the status that refused it, and the boot records how the farm came back — `load:local-existing`, `load:local-new`, `load:online-cached from …`. That last one alone separates "my farm reset" from "my farm loaded fine and something else went wrong", which no previous report could. On the online side the queue records its **pauses and resumes with the reason** — at `setPaused`, the single place `paused` is ever assigned, so no path can pause without the report saying which one — plus retries that never reach a pause, and whether a **recovery is still armed**. "Gameplay paused — reconnect to continue" was reported twice on connections that were demonstrably fine, and the pastes could not say which of a dozen paths paused it, whether anything resumed, or how long it had been stuck; the report now carries `gameplay: unavailable (state_conflict/q3)` and `recovery: retry armed (attempt 3)` — or `recovery: STALLED: nothing pending`, which is the invariant that class of bug breaks, stated in one line.
- **The writer lease is on the trail, including the drift that strands a farm for good.** The lease credential lives in sessionStorage; the client key it was issued against lives in localStorage. When the second is rebuilt while the first survives — an evicted key on iOS, a site-data clear with the tab still open — the two disagree, `POST /commands` answers 400 `bad_writer_command`, and because a rejected batch is retried verbatim the farm never recovers. Nothing said so, and nothing could: the batch is correctly fenced, the lease is live and being renewed by the other writer-protected routes, the account version simply stops moving, and every symptom points at a network that is fine. The report now carries the availability chain **in the order it fails** — `writer: lock held, credential held, identity DRIFTED` — plus crumbs for the browser lock not being granted (another tab is alive, which silently suppresses this document's credential for its whole life and otherwise surfaces only as a three-letter fragment), the lease being claimed or taken over, another device replacing it, and a clean release. The drift is crumbed **once**, not once per batch. What it deliberately does NOT carry: the clientId, the writer token, or the account id — all three are in scope at those call sites, and a test asserts none of them reaches the trail, because the player pastes this into a public bug report.
- **A repeating step collapses instead of filling the trail.** Retry loops are the normal shape of the failures worth reporting — a rejected batch resends on a backoff, a broken connection fails a texture per asset — and forty copies of one step would push out the steps that explain it. A repeat becomes `save:local 3kB (x6 over 4s)`, keeping both ends of the run, and the next step's gap is measured from when the repeating **stopped** rather than when it began.
- **A texture that will not load is counted instead of swallowed.** `loadTex` — the loader behind every texture a battle draws — catches everything and returns `null`, and every caller carries on without it. That is right (a missing decoration must not cost the player the fight) and it is also how a request for a file that could never exist went unseen through an entire beta: invisible from inside the game *and* from the bug report. Failures are now tallied into a `assets:` line — `none`, or `2 failed (first …, latest …)` — and the first couple leave a crumb of their own where they happened. Deliberately a tally rather than a crumb apiece: a battle entered with no connection fails a texture per asset, and thirty crumbs would push the trail that *explains* them straight out of the ring. Same local-only contract as the rest of the report, plus one more: a crumb is a fixed tag and a short structural detail — a catalog name, a same-origin asset path, a count — never save data, an account id, a token, or anything the player typed. The Settings note beside the button says what it copies, and SECURITY.md now states the contract a new crumb has to keep.

### Combat presentation

- **Arrrnold now slams when he actually hits you.** Two symptoms, one cause. Against a Headless front row the Pirate Boss raised both arms overhead, held them there for about four seconds, then slammed — and the slam that was drawn was not the one that dealt the damage; the damaging blow landed silently at the top of the raise, two and a half seconds earlier. Then, whenever the front row died and refilled, or a knockback shoved his target in and out of reach, he fired off a burst of full slams in half a second while his real attack rate never changed. The renderer was deriving where a rig sits in its swing as `1 - timerMs / cooldownMs`, and neither half of that holds. The timer is armed by `BattleSim.cycleMs`, which is `cooldownMs` only for a front-rank zombie and a non-mirroring enemy: **Arrrnold mirrors the body he faces** (his tuned 6.5 s slam against a Headless) off a 2.5 s dex clock, so the ratio was NEGATIVE for the first four seconds of every cycle and clamped flat at zero — the freeze — with the whole animation crammed into the last 2.5 s. And progress zero means *a blow just landed*, so the fraction before the contact frame draws that blow's follow-through; an enemy that has only just found something to hit has no blow behind it, and the sim re-arms an idle enemy's clock every tick, so each re-engage replayed the slam from nothing. Both readings are now taken from what the sim publishes rather than assumed: the cycle is whatever the attack timer was ARMED with, and the follow-through is drawn only when `struckThisTick` says a blow really landed inside it. **The same defect was hiding on the player side** — a zombie behind the front five is armed with a lineup band (x1.425/x2/x4), so it stood frozen on its contact pose for up to three quarters of every cycle — and it is fixed by the same reading. Presentation only: nothing here feeds back into the fight, and `attackPhase.test.ts` runs the real simulation and asserts the arm is overhead on the tick each blow lands, that one slam is drawn per blow and none without one, and that the arms never park mid-swing.
- **The Pirate Boss's slam is re-timed to match.** With the freeze gone the raise had to carry the whole cycle honestly, and the authored source timeline would not: it was a two-key smoothstep that eased OUT into the top, so the arms were within 10 degrees of overhead for the last fifth of the swing — a dwell that scales with the clock, which on a 6.5 s slam is over a second of standing still. The raise is now an ease-IN that climbs steadily and reaches the top only at the contact frame, so the slam leaves the top the instant it arrives. The loop seam is fixed with it: the source jumped straight from the slammed pose back to rest, teleporting both arms 135 degrees one frame after the hit, and the lift back out of the slam is now drawn — inside the post-contact tail, which is what puts the rig's rest pose at clip time zero, where the renderer parks a swing that has not struck yet. Contact stays at 0.95 and every pose angle is unchanged; only when the rig passes through them moved.


- **Every other swing in the game had the same two faults; they are fixed together.** The Pirate Boss was the case that got noticed, not the only one, so the whole set was swept. **Four more rendering paths** were still reading progress the broken way. The five Video Games actors and all eight Epic Bosses drive a FRAME STRIP through the same rotation a rig clip uses, so they carried the same post-contact tail and replayed it at nothing on every re-engage — and a strip is mostly tail, so that is most of the animation: Zedzox connects at 0.33 and Dr Groundhog at 0.25, which is two thirds of a four-frame swing and three quarters of a twelve-frame one, fired off every time the front row died and refilled. **Five more authored clips** ended their loop somewhere other than where they started it, which on a source timeline is a teleport one frame after every hit, forever: the Swashbuckler's arm jumped 135 degrees out of its slam (Arrrnold's own animation, one rank down, with all three of his faults), BroBot's windmill a quarter turn, the Ninja Girl's stab 90, and the Scallywag and Ninja Boss their back arms and heads. **And three of them had the blow landing on the wrong pose entirely.** The Ninja Girl's stab reached full extension at the far end of the cycle while the damage landed at the half-way mark with her arm still WOUND BACK; the Scallywag and the Ninja Boss connected with the arm already returned to rest, having whacked a moment earlier at nothing. Those swings now arrive on the beat the damage does. The same faults remain in the transcribed pose four more rigs share with the Scallywag — both alien units, the Crazed Worker and the Ninja Boy — which is a deliberate hold: fixing them means diverging from the binary on rigs nobody has authored a clip for, and that is a call to make on purpose rather than in passing. Nothing here touches the fight: `attacks.json` damage timings are ground truth and unchanged, every authored pose angle is unchanged, and only WHEN the rig passes through them moved.
- **A waiting enemy no longer freezes mid-lunge.** Found while sweeping the above, and it is the same fix aimed the other way. Holding an un-struck swing at the end of its follow-through is right for an animation that HAS one — but the procedural swing most enemies use has none: it rests over the first 28% of its cooldown and swings across the rest, so its rest pose is at the START. Held at the far end anyway, Old McDonnell stood frozen 98% of the way through his thrust, arm fully extended, from the moment he engaged until his first blow landed — the Farmhand at 76%, and the Tree World raccoon's second swing at 28%. The rule is now stated once, in terms of what a pose actually is rather than where it came from — the hand-transcribed family swings are on the source timeline and keep their tail, the cooldown-window ones have none and are never held — and a test walks every rig the raids field and checks each one lands at its own rest pose, whichever kind it is.
- **A knocked-back zombie now looks shoved instead of looking like it walked off.** Three separate things were telling the eye it had chosen to leave. It turned around: facing was read off velocity, and a shove is a large NEGATIVE velocity, so the rig spun to face the way it was flying and strode away from the fight with its back turned. It walked: the walk cycle ran over the slide, which is what sells self-propulsion more than anything else. And it moved at a constant speed, which reads as travel, where a shove is violent on impact and settles. It now stays squared up to the enemy that hit it, holds its standing pose, and is DRAWN on an ease-out — twice the speed off the mark, decelerating into the landing. **The simulation is untouched.** How far a zombie is shoved and how long it spends out of melee is ground truth (`knockBackBy: -(50 + arc4random() % 100) force: 5.0`) and the server re-runs it, so the slide's distance and duration are a rule, not a look; only the curve it is drawn on is ours. Both curves agree exactly at the start and the end of the slide, so nothing snaps on the way in or out. The "and then it gets stunned" that followed was the same bug's second half: having walked away, the zombie stopped dead and stood there until its attack timer came round.

- **Three bosses now stand on the thing they are standing on.** Found by watching them in the Raid Lab, which is the first time anyone had looked at these three side by side. Felix Wonky floated a little above the Valentine's shop counter — down a tenth of his height and his feet are on it. Goffy was up in the Tree World canopy overlapping the hanging monkey cages, which read as a bug in the cages rather than in him — down just under a full height and he stands on the mound at the foot of the tree. Zedzox was both oversized and hanging off the gatehouse: every boss fits to the same `BOSS_H`, but it is the rig's rendered bounds that land on screen, and his came out 174px where Felix and Goffy are both 161 — 8% over, which against pixel-art masonry reads as a giant. He is now scaled to just under the shared size and nudged onto the arch, so his robe hem and shoe rest on stone instead of overhanging open air. Occluding that leg behind the structure — the trick every other perch raid uses — is not available here: the Video Games perch layer is a 55px strip, and the measurement says pushing him far enough right to hide behind it takes his face off the right edge of the screen first.

- **Enemy arms no longer come apart mid-swing.** Every authored attack animation — the Swashbuckler's cutlass, the Lawyer's briefcase, the Crazed Worker's wind-through, the Pirate Boss's slash, the Corporate Boss's flail pair, the Ninja's stab, BroBot's spins, the Ringmaster, the unicycle bear — rotated each arm PART about that part's own anchor. A rig's "arm" is usually two or three parts (the upper arm and whatever it is holding), anchored tens of pixels apart, so each one set off on its own arc: the cutlass left the hand, the briefcase left the lawyer, and the assembly dismembered itself in mid-air before snapping back. The rear arm's counter-swing had the same fault. The slam already carried a note about exactly this ("Rotating at the sprite anchors made the Pirate Boss's arms detach and orbit") and already rotated about the authored `shoulder`; every other pose now goes through the same helper, using `shoulder` for the front assembly and the `back-shoulder` pivot ~12 rigs have carried unused since the pivot pass. A rig that authors no pivot for a side keeps the old in-place rotation, which is right for a one-part arm. Presentation only — the simulation never reads a bone.
- **The audit that followed found one more, and cleared everything else.** Measured across all 32 enemy rigs and all 55 zombie rigs: wheels are anchored at their hub (0.5, 0.5), wings at their root, zombie arms and the crop-arm mutations at the shoulder, zombie feet at the ankle — every one of those turns about the right point already. The one remaining broken assembly was the Ninja girl's **rear** arm: two parts, no authored `back-shoulder`, so it fell back to per-part anchors and separated by up to 38 px mid-stab. The rear side now takes the same fallback the front already had — the top-most part of the assembly — which for a one-part rear arm is that part's own anchor, i.e. no change for any other rig.
- **This is what the Rig Studio was built to find**, and it found it the first time anyone watched these animations on their own instead of glimpsing them in a two-minute fight. `src/rigClips.test.ts` now runs the real `EnemyActor` and the studio's transcription of it side by side over a whole attack cycle, every rig, every clip, and fails naming the part and the pixel gap — so the bench cannot drift from the game, and the game cannot drift back.

### Tooling

- **Capturing a review shot no longer throws away the thing you were reviewing.** `/__capture` writes into `tmp/review-shots/`, which is inside the project root — so the dev server was watching it and doing a full page reload on every shot. That turned both review tools against themselves: the whole point of capturing mid-session is to compare the next shot with this one, and each capture discarded the state the comparison needed — the selected rig, the running fight, any console instrumentation. Nothing under `tmp/` is ever imported, so nothing there should invalidate a module; the dev server now ignores it.
- **A Raid Lab, for watching the animations the Rig Studio authors actually play.** The Studio poses a rig against a timeline; nothing said what that rig does mid-invasion, and finding out meant fighting until it happened. A boss special fires on a weighted roll behind a cast timer and competes with every throw for the same slot; a Garden heal needs somebody hurt; a revive needs somebody dead; the Circus trapeze sweeps in once every few seconds into a fight you have to survive first. `raid-lab.html` (dev server, alongside `zombie-review.html`) runs a **real** `RaidScene` over a **real** `BattleSim`, built from the same `raid/fightConfig.ts` builders the game uses, and adds only a way to ask. Activated moves — Bash, Smash, Explode, Ver.2, Mini Buddy — go through the simulation's own `activate()`, live, and say plainly when nobody is in a position to perform one. Boss actions and the two rescue hazards are **soloed**: pick one and the fight is rebuilt carrying that alone on a short recovery, so it loops — the action keeps its authored cast time and its elite scaling, and only the mix and the recovery belong to the lab. Heals, revives and deaths are staged by **wounding** units and letting the simulation reach those states down its ordinary paths, so what plays is the real animation rather than a pose; nothing here writes a unit's state directly. Every playable invasion, every seasonal event and all eight Epic Bosses are in the picker at any level, wave roll and elite profile; "Skip to contact" runs off the ten-to-fifteen-second walk-in, Step and Step ×10 advance one 50 ms tick, and the speed slider reaches 0.05× for reading a single swing. `ZFLAB` in the console holds the scene, the sim and a hand frame-driver for anything the buttons do not cover.
- **The fight a boss brings is derived in one place now.** Seven builders — the projectile table, the special-action list, the alien summon rota, the pixel-zombie template, the junk/carrot wall, the trapeze and the crab — were private methods on `RaidManager`, so the only way to obtain a faithful fight config was to own a `GameState`, a `ZombieField` and a cleared cooldown. Two other places wanted the same answers, and one of them had already grown its own transcription: the elite balance test, whose whole job is to measure the fight the player gets, was measuring four configs it had written out by hand. They now live in `raid/fightConfig.ts` as pure functions of the assets, and the manager, the balance test and the lab all call them. A test pins the split (every authored boss action reaches the fight as either a throw or a special — a name falling between the two would be an action the boss silently never performs) and pins the single copy, by failing if `RaidManager` reads `bossActions` again.

- **The sprite assembler is now the Rig Studio — one bench, five tabs, for everything the raid screen draws.** `tools/rig_studio.html` (built by `tools/build_rig_studio.py`) contains the old rig editor whole, under the same storage key, and adds four tabs around it. **Animation** runs and edits the procedural animations that pose the rigs; **Epic Bosses** plays and retimes the eight bosses' authored frame strips; **Effects** fires the ability visuals at real stage scale; **Tiles** hosts `tile_lab.html` itself in a frame, so the flat-tile bench is a tab here without a second copy of its 1,200 lines. `tools/sprite_assembler.html` and its builder are retired rather than left to drift — two tools writing the same `zfsa:models:*` key is how a rig edit gets lost.
- **Every rig arrives already animated, from the game's own code rather than from a guess.** Opening a rig builds its real clip set out of `EnemyActor.ts` and `RaidActor.ts`: idle, move, one clip per *named* attack the rig actually swings (read off `enemy_stats.json`, fitted to that attack's `damageTiming` from `attacks.json`), and — for a boss — its 550 ms throw and its 3 s special, both of which the renderer drives off the same swing envelope. Where a rig has a hand-authored ZFAttackAnims timeline the timeline is transcribed key-for-key and flagged `authored` — the Crazed Worker's 180° wind-through, the Corporate Boss's two-strike Flail2, the Ninja's full-body stab, BroBot's two independent arm spins, the unicycle bear, the Midget stack, the Ringmaster; everything else gets the generic chop/jab/slam. The engine's piecewise-smoothstep envelopes come across as *exact* keys, because the tool's default ease is the engine's own `smooth`. 511 clips over 87 rigs, every one of which moves.
- **A clip is tracks, and a track carries its own timing.** One track poses one part GROUP (`arm.front`, `head`, `leg.back`, `body.2`, `root`) on one channel — rotation in degrees, translation in rig px, scale as a multiplier — with its own keys, its own ease, and, for a rotation, its own PIVOT. That last field is the one that matters: rotating the front arm about the rig's `shoulder` is what carries the weapon *with* the arm instead of spinning it on its own anchor, and it is why the arm can chop while the body holds still. Sine motions (the idle bob, the leg stride, the wing flap) stay expressed as waves until you bake them, so they read as what the engine does rather than as forty keys.
- **You pose on the stage, not in a void.** Every tab that shows a rig can put a real raid background behind it, laid out the way `RaidScene` does: the source 480×320 cocos design space, contain-fit, ground line at 0.9 of the stage height, units fitted to their role height (91 / 130 / 195 at the reference stage scale) with their feet on the line. Scrub the timeline and drag a part to key a move, Shift+drag to key a rotation; a hit marker on the ruler shows where in the clip the blow actually lands, which for a generic swing is *not* where the timeline starts (the engine rests for 28% of the cooldown, then swings). Clips save to the browser as you work and download as JSON.
- **A rig arriving mid-attack is still breathing.** The engine runs the whole-body bob and the head rock EVERY frame and only then lets an attack pose the rig on top, so those two never stop — and they are free-running on their own clocks, which no keyframe on an attack's timeline can express. Tracks marked `free` ride the wall clock instead, exactly as the engine does. Without them an attacking rig stood dead still with one arm flailing, which is not what the game shows. A PUNCH rig's front arm also hangs at its side whenever nothing else is posing it, so idle and move carry that droop now.
- **The Epic Boss backdrops no longer shake.** Their catalogs mark layers `isScrolling` and `isMoving`, and I read both `speed` fields as pixels per second. For the moving layers it is a DURATION IN MILLISECONDS per leg of the loop — Dr. Groundhog's 5 px bob is `speed: 200` — so "200 px/s across 5 px" came out as a 20 Hz jitter and the whole backdrop boiled. Scrolling layers are px/s but wrap over the authored TWO-tile pattern, not a fixed screen width. Both are fixed, and the toggle is off by default because **the game draws every level asset statically**: `RaidLevelAsset` carries only anchor/position/sprite/z. What the switch previews is what the source data asks for and the reimplementation does not yet do.
- **Stored edits announce themselves.** The studio restores your saved rigs and clips over the freshly-inlined ones, so re-running the builder after an art change could look like it did nothing. The tab bar now says how many stored edit sets are in the browser and offers to discard them.
- **The editing is where you can reach it.** The Animation tab had the controls but hid them: you had to know to click a timeline row's label, and the pivot an arm swings about — the single biggest lever on how an attack reads — lived on a different tab. Now clicking a part on the stage selects it and shows its target, its live rotation at the playhead as a number you can type into, and which joint it swings about; a green handle appears on that joint to drag the rotation; and the rig's own pivots are listed and draggable right there, writing through the same store the Rig tab uses. Clicking a part no longer silently adds empty tracks to the clip, and rig space now centres the rig's BOUNDS rather than its origin — enemy rigs are authored entirely in positive x, so zooming in used to push the body off the right-hand edge.
- **An enemy can be put where the invasion actually puts it.** A new *Place* control, next to *Role*: `auto` reproduces what the raid does with this rig — a boss goes on the structure the stage authors for it, worked out the way `RaidScene.computePerch` does (the tallest right-side layer, biased left of its centre, sunk below its top edge, plus that raid's own tweak), with the building drawn back over it so it occludes the legs exactly as the game layers it; a wave enemy stands in the doorway it holds at (field x 940); a zombie stands on its own staging line. The individual lane marks — spawn, hold, frontline, perch, staging — are selectable one at a time, and the badge names whichever it used. Raids that author no right-side structure keep the default sky perch, which is what makes the Alien saucer and the Beach squid read as hovering.
- **The overlays stopped sitting on the controls.** The stage badge and the hint were positioned against the whole centre COLUMN rather than the canvas, so they covered the toolbar's second row and the timeline's track labels. Both are inside the canvas now, and the hint has a × — it has a job on first open and none after.
- **The side panels scroll.** Every side column was a stack of panels — inspectors, the pivot editor, the track and key boxes, an export box — inside a fixed-height column with `overflow: hidden`, so on a short window the lower panels were squashed and then cut off with no way to reach them. The columns scroll now; panels keep their natural height, only the panes with their own scrollbar give ground (down to a floor where they are still usable), and each section heading sticks to the top so you can see which panel you are in. Same fix in the Tile Lab, which had the same stack.
- **A pivot added on the Rig tab shows up on the Animation tab.** The two tabs share one model object, so the new joint was really there — but the Animation tab's cross-tab hook only re-drew the canvas. The pivot LIST and the "swings about" selects are built from `pivotsOf(model)`, and nothing rebuilt them, so the pivot stayed invisible until something else happened to (switching clips, which rebuilds everything). Both tabs now re-read the rig when they come on screen, which also fixes the other direction: a pivot nudged from the Animation tab's own rows no longer leaves stale numbers on the Rig tab.
- **The Rig and Animation tabs share one selected rig.** They are two views of the same thing — one edits the skeleton, the other edits what the skeleton does — and holding separate selections meant an edit made on one tab was invisible on the other purely because the other was pointed at a different unit, which reads exactly like a bug. Picking a rig on either tab now moves both, across a dataset switch too, and the Animation tab drops its rig filter rather than pointing at a name the filter hides. The Effects tab keeps its own two selects, since it needs a player rig *and* an enemy rig at once.
- **An animation edited in the Rig Studio now runs in the game.** The studio could always transcribe a rig's motion onto a timeline and let it be edited, but an edited clip had nowhere to land: the export was the deliverable and applying it meant hand-porting keyframes back into `EnemyActor`. It lands now — drop the Animation tab's download in as `assets/raids/enemies/clips.json` and the actor poses itself from the clip. A rig with no authored clip is untouched and runs exactly the code it always ran, which is the whole safety argument: the substitution is a no-op until the clip differs, and the conformance test pins it from both ends — installing the built-in clip (the transcription of the procedural code) must change no pose anywhere, and an edited clip must actually move the rig. It caught a real sign error in the new path while being written: the clip authors the attack lunge already carrying the facing sign, and negating it again sent the Farmhand 9px the wrong way. Clips are cosmetic — `BattleSim` does not import the actors — so no ruleset moves. **Enemies only so far**: the zombie `RaidActor` and a perched boss's `throw`/`ability` still run procedurally.
- **The clip evaluator moved into the app.** `tools/rigClips.js` was the one copy of the schema, the evaluator and the built-in clips; the evaluator half is now `src/raid/rigClips.js` — the runtime's own file, which the studio inlines and the test drives — and the built-ins stay behind in `tools/rigClipsAuthored.js`, out of the game bundle, since a rig with no authored clip never needs them.
- **The zombies too — and the coverage that had to come first found a real bug.** Wiring `RaidActor` the same way meant trusting its clip transcription, and unlike the enemy rigs nothing had ever checked it: the conformance test was enemy-only. It was wrong. `partMeta` splits a zombie's head into head/eye/jaw so a bite can open the jaw and squint the eyes on their own tracks, but `RaidActor` hangs the whole face off the neck and rocks and lunges it AS ONE — so every track aimed at plain `head` moved the skull and left the eyes and jaw behind. - **The game asks the browser to stop throwing its storage away.** Reported as settings not surviving a browser restart, with no site data ever cleared by hand — and nothing in the writing code can produce that: every setting is a plain `localStorage` write, and the value is provably still there on a reload. This game is an unusually strong candidate — the service worker runtime-caches roughly 88 MB of artwork — and an *online* player would see precisely what was reported and nothing more, because the farm itself comes back from the server; only the device-local half goes. - **"Gameplay paused — reconnect to continue" no longer means reload the page.** Reported by two players on a connection that was demonstrably fine, and the live account data agreed with them: the writer lease was held and being renewed, the session was nowhere near its idle timeout, no operation was stuck — and the account version had not moved in the best part of an hour. A live lease applying zero batches. The command queue can be paused from about a dozen places, and each one was responsible for arming its own retry; **several of them didn't**. A version conflict pauses through a path that emits no "unavailable" event, so it schedules nothing and hands recovery entirely to a reload handler — whose own request runs over the same network the batch just failed on, and which did nothing but show a toast when it failed. It is — and it is one effect built twice, from the same classes with byte-identical numbers in `-[ZombieActorGarden ressurectZombie:]` and `-[ZFFightMan summonBoss:]`: an opaque white layer 100 wide and 320 tall (320 being the *whole screen*, not a sprite — the iPad branch asks for 640, its own full height), standing on the bottom edge centred on the actor, opening from nothing over 0.2 s and then closing back to nothing over 1.3 s while it fades. It shrinks from both sides because a cocos2d layer is positioned from its bottom-left but *scaled* about its centre, and it sits behind the unit it delivers — in front, a full-height opaque bar would white out the very thing it is announcing. Both sites also play `resurrect.wav`, which the reimplementation has shipped unused since the audio was extracted; the abductee's arrival now uses the resurrection cue too, exactly as the original does. Full derivation in `docs/mechanics/ZOMBIE_ABILITIES_BINARY_AUDIT.md`.
- **Abducted humans stand on the scorch mark at every window size, not just one.** Found while placing the pillar above. The correction that centres an abductee on its mark was written in the unit's own local coordinates and used directly as a screen offset, so it was only right when the stage happened to be drawn at 1:1. On a narrow phone-shaped viewport it dragged the abductee a third of the stage to the left, into the zombies' waiting crowd. The same unit mismatch is fixed in the (currently disabled) zombie laser beam, which took three token-local measurements the same way.
- **Mini Buddy can only be used on a brute that is actually being deployed.** The button armed from the first frame of the fight and a tap landed, loading the mini onto whichever carrier was furthest back in the queue — a zombie nowhere near the deployment slot. The move is meant for the moment a Large stands in that slot with its focus bar filling, which is the one moment the player is deciding about *that* zombie, and it now takes exactly that: the carrier must have reached the slot, not merely have left the back group (the walk out is most of that state), and there must still be a mini waiting behind it. The button's look and the tap it accepts are now one and the same test, so a lit button always means a tap will land — Mini Buddy has no cooldown, so unlike every other move it has no "recharging" state to keep it on screen through. Measured on a 20-strong army: it now arms 5.5 s into the fight, on the Large standing in the slot, and 1054 taps outside that window are refused where before any of them would have been taken. **Raid ruleset 30** — this refuses taps the sim used to accept, and a refused tap is dropped rather than fatal on the finish path, so without the bump the two simulations would quietly settle different fights.
- **The army's health bar starts full.** Reported as a stripe of the player's team bar showing dark from the first frame with every zombie at full health — and it was the bar that was wrong, not the army. The bar divided the live HP total by a figure captured when the scene was built, from the roster the combat builder hands over; that roster's Life already includes the full team aura (Chivalry, Grace, Fortitude), while the battle pays an aura only to the zombies that have actually marched onto the field. So before the first one deployed, every carrier's real maximum sat below the number the bar was measuring against. Measured on a 20-strong specials army with a level-40 farm, it opened at 83% — the missing sixth was the aura for zombies still waiting to walk in, which the bar had already counted as theirs. Both bars now read against totals the battle itself owns, so numerator and denominator always describe the same units. Two things fall out of that: a fallen zombie keeps its maximum in the total, so a loss cannot refill the bar by shrinking what it is measured against, and a boss's wall or an abducted human no longer counts its HP into the enemy bar — that had pinned it at full for as long as one stood, while both keep their own bars on the field. The head counts are unchanged and still tally everything standing.
- **A zombie lost to an Epic Boss is remembered by the Memorial Statue.** Reported as "special zombies can't be placed on the statue" — the zombie was a special (Brock Coley), but nothing about the *zombie* was involved: the statue never remembered anyone lost to a boss. The graveyard is server-owned (`fallen_v3`, migration 0047) precisely so a visitor sees the same occupant the owner does, and the invasion settlement wrote a row for every casualty at the one moment a zombie stops existing. The Epic Boss settlement deleted the roster row and wrote nothing. The client did record the loss locally, so the statue offered it until the next bootstrap replaced the graveyard with the authoritative one — and from then on the farm reported no losses at all. Both settlements now bury their dead on the same terms, cap included. Migration 0053 recovers the ones already lost: a finished session's stored replay config carries the party the *server* built, so unit id, catalog key, mutation mask and inherited tint come back exactly; the name reverts to the deterministic default (names live only in the client's presentation blob, keyed by the roster id that went with the zombie) and veterancy reads 0, since it is folded into the scaled stats and cannot be divided back out. Epic losses still have no revival offer — that is unchanged, and a memento was never a way back.
- **Invasion placement pass.** From a playtest: the wave now holds 25 units further right, and the front rank closes with it, so the two lines meet nearer the doorway instead of out in the field. Garden healers no longer hang off the front line by a fixed setback — in this sim's deeper formation that dropped them into the milling crowd at about a sixth of the way across — and instead hold a fixed support station at 3/10 of the visible stage, still well clear of the combat band (which is what keeps them off the alien laser's target list). And an abducted human is now placed by where it is DRAWN: enemy rigs anchor at their model-space left edge, which reads fine for a crowd in a doorway and badly for one unit standing alone on a mark, so a summon is shifted left by its own half-width and its figure sits centred on the scorch. Per unit, not a fixed nudge — the five abductees are different widths. Ruleset change, folded into the alien bump below.
- **Old McDonnell's woodsman stops knocking you back on every swing.** A unit does not have "an attack with knockback" — it rolls one entry out of its `attacks` list per swing and applies *that* entry's flags. This had collapsed the list to a single boolean, which handed every swing the rarest entry's effects: the Lumberjack's shove lives on `LumberjackSpecial`, 10 of his 100 frequency, and he was shoving on all 100. He now shoves about one hit in ten, as authored. The same fix moves the Lawyers boss's stun to its authored 40% and the Special/Tree World/Valentine's minions 2 and 3 to 50%; every other carrier is already a 100% entry and is unchanged. Ruleset change, folded into the alien bump below.
- **Zombies vs Aliens plays like the original.** A tester listed six faults; five were real, and disassembling the stage again turned up the same four hard-coded special cases the source has for raid 6 (write-up in `docs/mechanics/ALIEN_RAID_RECOVERED.md` §7). **The invasion is a SWARM** — the fight keeps one "current" enemy plus a five-slot `enemySlots` array, six at once, and stage 6 is the only one whose `spawnTimer` is seeded to 10 s instead of an hour, so it is the only raid whose slots are ever filled. One more alien walks out every ten seconds up to six; the other ten invasions stay one-at-a-time, exactly as before. **Every alien is a different colour** — the minion art in the atlas is greyscale on purpose (measured saturation 0-2, against the boss's already-purple parts) because each one is tinted from three random channels at spawn; the face and body detail are the two parts flagged `setInheritColor: NO` and stay grey. **The boss's summon abducts a HUMAN**, not another alien: a rota of lumberjacks, a crazed worker, a ninja boy, a farmhand and a swashbuckler, refilled by a five-way roll on every cast so it never runs dry, with only one abductee alive at a time and none of them counting against the wave. They are beamed into the **middle of the stage** (the only spawn in the game that branches on the device — 240 of a 480-wide phone stage, 480 of a 960-wide tablet one, i.e. the centre either way) and stand there, so they are not something the army walks up to: they are a roadblock across the lane, and the whole army has to stop and clear one before it can go on, for as long as the boss keeps casting. **The saucer is dropped the moment the boss lands** — it is attached while he is perched and destroyed outright when he touches down, so the ground phase is fought on foot, and the idle hover goes with it (`startAnim:` guards the bob on the boss still *having* a ship, so the alien stops bobbing once he is standing on the ground). **And a landed boss has no specials at all**: the action roll only exists in the perched state, which also stops Zedzox casting turnZombie for the whole ground phase of the Video Games invasion. The sixth report — the boss's health bar showing before he descends — is faithful: he is on stage in his saucer from the first frame, and a bar is attached to every actor the moment it spawns. **Raid ruleset 26 -> 27**: an invasion in flight at deploy time settles as `stale_ruleset` and pays nothing. Elite profiles for the Aliens and the Video Games are re-fitted in the same change, because both raids' difficulty moved under the rules above. **Two playtest verdicts ride along**, both recorded where a data regeneration cannot undo them: the basic alien's strength drops 6 → 5 (nothing is wrong with 6 in isolation — it is a mid-table minion — but the recovered swarm delivers it six times over onto the one front zombie, which is twice the incoming rate any other invasion can produce; 4 was tried first and read as too soft, an elite run clearing with no losses at all), and the abductee now lands on the scorch mark rather than on the stage's arithmetic centre, which sits a little to its right.
- **Pond pieces lay together into one pond.** Two separate faults, both visible the moment you put two pieces side by side. Pond 5 came back ~6 px out of line when turned: the seven pieces are one interchangeable set (six rims and a fill, all 3x3 on the same 150x75 canvas) and the SE and NW rims are the SW and NE ones turned round, but their source pivots are hand-rounded to 2dp and disagree — Pond 5 0.36, Pond 2 and Pond 7 0.35, the rest 0.34 — and turning is a mirror about the front tile's centre line, which doubles the disagreement. In the original this never showed because ponds are not turned there. All seven now take the anchor the geometry fixes (0.34, 0.02), which is also the mirror's own fixed point, so a piece occupies identical pixels either way round. Separately, every join drew a thin dark line: the source atlases store colour premultiplied by alpha and PixiJS premultiplies image textures again on upload, so each piece's antialiased edge — pieces overlap by 3 px by design — composited at `rgb*a²` over the neighbour's water instead of `rgb*a`. The flat-tile art (ponds, both road sets, rocks, the zombie patch) now ships straight-alpha, which is what the renderer already assumed, and the water blends into water. Roads keep their authored pivots: a bend's art is deliberately off-centre on its footprint, so the pond reasoning does not apply to it.
- **The battle strip's ability buttons stay on screen on a phone held in landscape.** An army with everything unlocked put five buttons in a single vertical column, 64 px apart, under a top HUD that starts it ~136 px down — so on a 375 px-tall landscape viewport the last two hung off the bottom with no way to reach them. Explode and Explode Ver.2 now share a button, showing whichever move a tap would actually spend and a badge counting the zombies ready for either — a zombie carrying both tiers counts once, not twice. The slots themselves still never move or re-pack. Sharing that button costs nothing, because Ver.2 is the same 10× blast that can also hit a boss: there is no fight in which you would rather spend the base one, so the button always spends the upgrade while any carrier is ready for it. Not a ruleset change — the button still sends one concrete move key, and it is one the sim accepts, so recorded transcripts replay exactly as before.
- **Bash and Smash get a button each — the choice between them is yours again.** They were briefly stacked the same way, and that was wrong: Smash is not an upgrade of Bash, it is a different trade. Bash hits every enemy for 2.75× after a 2.44-cycle wind-up; Smash hits for 1.8× plus a 1-second area stun after 1.46. Per second of committed time Smash is about 9% ahead and stuns on top, but per 10-second recharge — the real throughput gate — Bash wins from two enemies on the field upward, because its extra 0.95× lands on every one of them. Stacking handed that judgement to the tier ladder and always spent Smash, and a veteran Large's Bash became unreachable for the rest of the game. Both buttons now stand on their own. A Large carrying both shows up ready on each and spends its cooldown on whichever you pick — one zombie, one move per ten seconds, not two. That makes four buttons the worst case, and the column's pitch now tightens to fit them (four at the authored spacing cleared a 375 px viewport but not the home indicator underneath it); a column of one or two keeps the spacing it always had.
- **Mini zombies no longer push to the front line and get killed.** Ruleset 23 brought over the binary's per-body standoff, in which the lightest body plants closest to the enemy — and this sim commits an enemy's entire output to the single front-most zombie, so that made every Mini in the army the designated casualty of every fight. A Mini now stands where a Regular stands (as do the Cupid Gardens that bucket with it); the Headless is still the one body that pushes forward, which is the whole point of a Headless. **Raid ruleset 23 -> 24**: an invasion in flight at deploy time settles as `stale_ruleset` and pays nothing.
- **Drag-plowing no longer skips plots.** The stroke laid every new plot on the lattice of wherever the finger went down, and dropped — silently — any square that collided with something. That lattice is not shared with the plots already on the farm, so a swipe running alongside an existing row failed for exactly the stretch beside it and worked at both ends: "3-4 pieces unplowed around the centre of my selection". Measured on a real save, the same swipe laid 12 plots or 5 depending only on which tile it started from, one row apart. A square that will not fit is now nudged to wherever it does fit *under the pointer*, so the run stays aligned where it can and never leaves a hole where there is room.
- **Queued farm work survives an outage instead of vanishing.** Online, `apply` refuses to mutate a plot while the command lane is paused — but the job was finished anyway, so every plot the farmer reached during a hiccup was quietly consumed. Jobs now wait and resume on their own, and the catch-up leaves early rather than grinding through the whole absence.
- **An invasion can be launched with a big plow/plant queue.** Plow and plant travel as bulk commands (`farm.plow_many` / `farm.plant_many`, up to a full 289-plot board each), folded together in the outbox. One command per plot could not fit through the Worker's 120-semantic-commands-per-minute budget, so a full-field pass left `settle()` — which every invasion launch waits on — draining behind 429s for minutes. Partial refusals are reported once with a plot count instead of one toast per plot.
- **Objects with writing in their art no longer rotate into gibberish.** "Rotate" is a horizontal mirror, which is a real quarter turn for isometric art but turns the Ice Cream Stand's sign into "MAERC ECI". The four objects with baked lettering (Ice Cream Stand, Ice Cream Truck, and both 2012 banners) refuse to turn and say why; a save that already stored one as turned comes back the right way round. Reviewed the whole object set — every raid banner is asymmetric and mirrors perfectly well, because its crest is a picture.
- **Mobile portrait zooms out much further.** The camera was forbidden from zooming past the point where the view is taller than the world, because above the backdrop there was nothing but green filler. That limit is slack in landscape and binding in portrait: a phone was pinned near 0.38 while the same farm reached 0.25 on a desktop. The height term is gone, and the surplus a tall view creates is now spent entirely **downward**. Sky was the wrong direction to solve it in: there is nothing above the backdrop to look at, whereas below the farm is more of the same ground it already stands on. So the top of the view is pinned to the top of the drawn art, and the decorated grass ring is grown down to cover everything the camera can drop to — which on a zoomed-out portrait phone is most of the screen. That far strip is drawn outside the depth-sorted entity layer, and can be: everything in it is strictly south of everything in that layer, so there is nothing to interleave, and the layer's sort is O(n²) in a count this would have quadrupled. It is also extended a row at a time rather than rebuilt, so a viewport that changes shape never regenerates the scenery already on screen — and because the strip's reach is measured worst case over both orientations, a rotate needs no new scenery at all. Each backdrop still gets a short band of its own top-row colour above the art, but only to close the seam left by scaling that art by a fractional factor. Landscape is unchanged (its width limit binds first).
- **The farmer's lantern can be switched off.** Tap him at night, or use Settings > Farmer's Lantern. Off puts the lamp away and leaves the farm lit only by whatever you have placed. Persisted per device.

### Windows downloads
- New **launcher** package (`launcher/`): unzip, double-click `Play Zombie Farm.cmd`, and the offline build opens in the default browser with a Desktop shortcut. Zero install — Windows PowerShell and the in-box C# compiler are enough. Serves `game/` from a loopback-only socket on a pinned port (saves are per-origin, so the port cannot drift), replaces the shipped service worker with a self-unregistering one, and sends everything `no-store` so modded files show up on reload.
- New **desktop app** (`desktop/`): `ZombieFarm.exe`, the same offline build in a Tauri window — no browser, no console, no local server, no port. A custom URI scheme serves `game/` off disk so mods keep working instead of being compiled into the binary.
- The desktop app answers `/sw.js` with a self-unregistering worker. v0.2.1 shipped without this, and the shipped build's CacheFirst service worker silently hid modded art: a replaced PNG kept serving its old bytes across reloads. A 404 would not do, since that leaves an already-installed worker in charge.
- Known limitation: that only protects a clean profile. WebView2 does not route service-worker script fetches through the custom protocol handler, so a profile that already ran v0.2.1 fails its `/sw.js` update check and keeps the stale worker; resetting it means deleting `%LOCALAPPDATA%\com.zombiefarmreforged.desktop`, which also deletes that farm. Documented in `HOW TO PLAY.txt` and `desktop/README.md`.
- `.github/workflows/release-windows.yml` publishes both zips on a `v*` tag. Both jobs refuse to package a bundle that still references the live Worker, so a moddable local client can never reach production.
- The two packages keep separate saves (a native webview gets its own storage); the app's instructions cover Export/Import.
- Both packages can now **check** for a newer release, and never install one. The shell writes `update.json` from the repository that built the zip, declares it to the game via `/__zfshell.js`, and Settings > Check for Updates asks that repository's releases. A player is asked before anything happens, and accepting only opens the download page — nothing on disk is touched, because overwriting `game/` would delete their mods. Nothing checks on launch or in the background: with no `update.json` the shell declares empty values, refuses `/__open-release`, and does not add `api.github.com` to `connect-src`, so a package without a channel cannot reach the network at all.
- Forks need no configuration for any of this: the update channel comes from `$GITHUB_REPOSITORY` at packaging time, so a fork's package offers the fork's own releases.

### Fixes (client)
- **A full friend-invasion defense reads 6/6, not 5/6 — and Girl is a job of its own.** The sixth slot could never be filled. A formation defense holds one zombie of each class and there are six classes, but two of them — Regular and Girl — held the same *job* on the field, both arriving as reinforcements on a timer. The picker enforced "one of each" by counting jobs, so those two classes competed for a single slot: choosing a Girl evicted the Regular already standing there, the counter stopped at five, and the sixth slot sat there looking like something the player had failed to earn. Nothing else agreed with it — the server has always accepted six (its duplicate check is per class), and the automatic strongest pick has always fielded six — so a player who left the defense on automatic got a line the editor would not let them build by hand. **The fix is to the model, not to the count:** Normal and Girl are now two jobs rather than one job with two holders, so the job list, the class list and the defense cap are the same six things and "one of each" is a single unambiguous rule wherever it is asked. They still do identical work — same station, same reinforcement beat, one shared drip counter — and the picker names them apart (Reinforcement (Normal), Reinforcement (Girl)) so an empty slot says which kind of zombie is missing. Deliberately NOT a ruleset change: the two share a front-to-back rank and still settle between themselves on unit id, so no fight in flight moves and every existing replay is byte-identical. A test pins both halves — six distinct jobs, and the two line jobs holding the same station and the same beat — so neither the count nor the sameness can drift again.
- **The X that closes a menu can no longer be sliced in half.** Spotted on the Almanac's field-note pages, and fixed for every menu rather than that one. A close button positioned OUTSIDE its panel's box is clipped the instant that panel becomes a scroll container, and panels on the shared modal layer become scroll containers all the time — Settings, the friends list, raid select, the Farmer's Guide and the Almanac's pages each outgrow the screen on their own. Each had been patched with its own "tuck it back in" override as the clipping was spotted, one panel at a time, which is exactly why the Almanac page shipped cropped: it was simply the one nobody had thought to patch yet. The shared `.panelclose` sits INSIDE the corner by default now, so no panel can crop it however it scrolls — the whole class of bug is impossible rather than merely catchable. The bound-and-scroll rule that creates the hazard also moved out of the touch layout onto the modal layer it belongs to: it was phone-only, so a short DESKTOP window — a laptop in landscape, a half-height browser — centred a tall panel with its heading and close button stranded above the viewport, which looks exactly like a cropped X. Three panels whose top-right corner holds a counter (the roster total, the Pot's timer, the army's slot count) reserve its width so the two cannot collide. The four hand-built market-parchment panels keep the authentic overhanging X and stay safe for a different reason — they are fixed-size flex columns whose inner lists scroll, so they never clip their own corner — and `ui/closeButtonFit.test.ts` fails if one of them ever grows an overflow, or if the shared button drifts back outside the box.
- **Every colour of fence holds — six of them never did.** Found in review, alongside the terrain-priced pathfinding above. A one-tile fence panel draws a rail about two tiles wide and bridges into the next tile, so a run laid every OTHER tile reads as one unbroken wall; the collision underneath only matches if the panel blocks that second tile too. Which panels got that overhang was a hand-written list of four keys — one per fence *family* — so **Pink, Blue, Red and Black Fence, Pink Iron Fence and Christmas Fence** drew a sealing panel's art while blocking a single tile each. Measured on the same 5-panel spaced run: the plain Fence left **0 gaps**, the Pink Fence **5**, one at every join, and the run looked identical. Nothing announced it, because the hole is exactly where the art overlaps. The set is derived now rather than listed — anything the terrain price list calls a barrier and the catalog gives a one-tile footprint is a panel, whatever it is named — and a test pins the resulting ten keys against the real catalog, so the next colour variant cannot arrive quietly. Multi-tile barriers are deliberately excluded: a 1x5 hedge already owns the five tiles it is drawn across, and bridging it into a sixth would be a phantom wall beside the fence rather than a seal. One visible consequence, and it is the correction rather than a side effect: those six now take the half-tile art drop every fence panel takes, which is what lands their end posts on the lattice corners, so an already-placed coloured run settles into line with its neighbours and meets a gate squarely.
- **Art that shipped BROKEN can now reach an install that already cached it.** The service worker runtime-caches `/assets/**.png` CacheFirst for 60 days, which is right for 88 MB of stable filenames and has one failure mode: correcting an asset *in place* never reaches anyone who loaded the bad one, because the URL is identical so the cache never asks the network. The fix waits out the expiry, per player. `artCacheRepair.ts` is an explicit, versioned eviction list — bumping its epoch re-runs a sweep once per browser that drops only the named files, so nothing else in the cache is re-downloaded. Epoch 1 evicts Worm Hole A and B, which shipped as the transparent 111x142 placeholder frame the extractor picked and were corrected on 2026-08-03. Epoch 2 evicts all fifteen flat tiles, whose seams are fixed above. It is deliberately not reached for when art merely *changed*: a redraw arriving late is fine, and this exists for the case where "late" means "never".
- Production CSP now allows `data:` in `connect-src`. PixiJS probes for worker ImageBitmap support by fetching a 1x1 data-URL PNG; blocking it made the probe report "unsupported", which silently moved every texture fetch and decode onto the main thread (202 of them during boot) and logged two CSP errors per page load.
- A battle scene that fails to load no longer strands the player. Both launch paths put the farm into battle mode before awaiting `RaidScene.create`, and every path back out lived inside the scene's own `onFinish` — so a scene that never built left `raidActive` true forever: blank screen, farm queue paused, HUD in raid layout, and only a reload to escape. Both chains now restore the farm, say what happened, and release the online session's fence.
- An online Epic Boss result no longer stores the run's timestamps in the *server's* clock. The finish handler wrote the raw projection straight back, undoing the conversion `adoptEpicBossResult` had just applied; the event window, the encounter timeout, the retry gate and Boss Token drops are all judged against `Date.now()`, so a client whose clock differed from the server's read them all wrong until the next bootstrap repaired it.
- The Profiles panel builds the signed-in display name with `textContent` instead of `innerHTML`, matching the rule the friends list and nameplate already follow. Nothing was exploitable — the server's username allowlist excludes markup — but the safety lived in a regex three modules away rather than at the sink.
- A failed Local Farm write cleans up its own scratch copy, so a quota failure does not leave a whole save's worth of storage behind for the retry to trip over.
- **A quest that arrives already finished no longer sticks on the rail unrewarded.** Completion was only ever tested inside an event that advanced a counter, and a requirement already at its target bails out before that — so no later event could re-run the check. Lowering a requirement does exactly this to every save holding a count at or above the new figure: Master Combiner went from 50 collections to 15, leaving "Collect 15 zombies from the Zombie Pot (15/15) ✓" parked in the log forever. Offline, restore and epic-event reactivation now sweep for satisfied quests and close them out, which can cascade into a successor. Online is untouched — the server owns completion there, re-checks every eligible quest on each batch, and self-completing locally would grant a reward it never agreed to.

### Fixes (server)
- The item shed's capacity is authoritative again. The retired v2 route enforced it (`planStore` via `shedCapacity`); protocol v3's `storage.move` dropped the check, leaving the cap client-side only, so an edited client could file any number of items into a Shabby Shed. Rejects with `shed_full`, derived from the placed shed's tier exactly as the client derives its own cap.
- `object.upgrade`'s free-starter-shed adoption — the one upgrade path that *inserts* a client-named object — now applies the same instance-id fence as `object.buy` and `storage.claim`. It previously accepted any 128-character string into a document other players read on farm visits.

### Combat
- Resurrect no longer refuses Small zombies — the old blanket rejection came from a misread binary note and stranded Smalls killed by ordinary enemies.
- **A revived zombie now comes back alive but SPENT.** The binary's own revival walks the returning zombie's ability list and marks every one-use ability consumed (`ressurectZombie:` 0x7d3c2–0x7d436), used or not — so an exploder pulled out of its own blast cannot light a second fuse, a bomber cut down before it lit one comes back without it, and a revived Garden zombie cannot pay the revive forward. An army therefore gets at most one revive per Garden zombie it deployed. The earlier "comes back whole" rule was a divergence written up as if it were the recovered behavior; `abilityRearms` and its back-of-the-queue tiebreak existed only to make that survivable and are gone.
- **Resurrect now draws from a corpse backlog instead of firing at the instant of death.** The source game appends the dead to a list that nothing drains but a revival, and the Garden zombie polls it from its own update — so a Garden zombie that reaches the field *after* a casualty still brings that casualty back, where before the corpse was lost for good. The target is the most recent casualty, and a holder carrying both Resurrect and Heal spends its beat on the revive (the two share one cast slot, revive checked first).
- The battle strip's Resurrect icon is now a **revives-left counter** across deployed Garden zombies, keeping its badge down to the last one and disappearing at zero.
- Raid ruleset bumped 20 → 22, then 25 → 26 for the two changes above.

### Epic Boss events
- **Every boss has a favourite crop, and harvesting it can start that boss's event for free.** The eight pairings are in `src/epicBoss/favoriteCrops.ts` and shown on each card in Market → Epic Boss: Dr. Groundhog/**Potatoes**, Bully Frog/**Venus Flytrap**, Rocky Rhino/**Broccoli** (its prize zombie is Brock Coley), General Larvaelus/**Garlic**, Mystical Mamba/**Lima Beans**, Foul Owl/**Dragon Fruit**, Skunkarella/**Pumpking**, Loco Locust/**Corpse Flower**. Two effects, and they never both apply — one is for while an event runs and the other for while none does. **During its own boss's event, a favourite crop yields Boss Tokens 25% more often** (`FAVORITE_CROP_TOKEN_BONUS`), applied outside the ceiling clamp rather than inside it, because three of the eight are 24-hour crops and the whole 24-hour band sits *on* that ceiling — folded in before the clamp the bonus would have been silently eaten on exactly the crops most likely to be planted for it. **With no event running, the harvest can instead lure that boss onto the farm and start its event outright, free**, provided the player has reached its unlock level; a locked boss's crop is a silent no-op rather than a fallback to some other boss, because which one you might draw is meant to be a planting decision. Three rules bind the pairings and are held by tests: a favourite crop never unlocks after its boss (so no event is gated behind produce you cannot buy), no crop belongs to two bosses (the token bonus keys off the *running* event, which a shared crop would make ambiguous), and every favourite grows for at least four hours — the lure rate is flat per PLOT-DAY, so a 15-minute crop would need a ~1-in-50,000 per-harvest chance to be worth the same per day, which is correct arithmetic and invisible feel. Seven of the eight are already mutation crops, which is the point: this rides on planting a developed farm already does.
- **The lure rate is 0.006 events per plot-day** of the crop (`EPIC_BOSS_START_RATE_PER_PLOT_DAY`), quoted for a 24-hour crop, with shorter grow times tilted gently below it — Broccoli's four hours earn 76% of a 24-hour crop's daily rate. Calibrated so that 75 plots of one favourite at 75% uptime draws an event about every three days, against a casual 8-plot patch at half uptime waiting about six weeks. 75 is a dedicated patch rather than a ceiling: the farm upgrades 30→40→50→60→70 and the server caps plots at `MAX_FARM_PLOTS` = 289 (derived and exact; the ~200 a real farm keeps after buildings and the zombie patch is an estimate, not a measurement), and the rate is linear in plot count — 40 plots draws one every 5.6 days, 200 plots one every 1.1. **What decides whether the feature is felt at all is the plot count where the expected wait drops under the 14 days an event lasts: about a dozen plots of one favourite** (11.9 for a 24-hour crop, 15.6 for Broccoli), saturating by thirty. The gate caps *frequency* at one per 14 days however large the farm, since a lure only rolls when no event is running; it does not cap *uptime*, and a 200-plot farm sits at 93% of the time with an event live. That is accepted rather than damped — but not on the token-supply argument this entry first made. "A running event is a grind gated by token supply, so a farm big enough to keep one live cannot afford attempts at it" does not survive the arithmetic: a ladder costs an ordinary army ~45 attempts and a favourite pays 0.11–0.33 tokens per plot-day, so that many arrive within one event at ~10 plots of Pumpking, ~12 of Garlic, ~29 of Potato — the same band the lure turns on in. Token supply stops binding right where events start overlapping, so what is really being accepted is the overlap itself, on the judgement that a permanently available grind is a fair return for a farm given over to one crop.
- **The lure is rolled by the server, unlike the Boss Token.** A token is worth one brain, which is why the client mints its own and merely reports them; a lure is worth the boss's whole activation price *and* reopens its prize quest chain, signature zombie included. The Worker rolls it while replaying the harvest it already grow-gates, so the roll costs nothing extra and cannot be minted by an edited client. A field-wide Insta-Harvest rolls every plot it pulls but can start only one event — the first hit leaves an event running and the rest refuse. Migration `0054` adds `epic_boss_runs_v3.started_crop`, which records the crop responsible and is how the client tells an event it was handed from one it bought.
- **An event that starts now says so.** A boss-portrait popup opens on activation, naming the crop when one lured it ("Your Corpse Flower harvest drew Loco Locust onto the farm") and stating the 14-day clock, the rung count, and where tokens come from. It exists mainly for the events nobody asked for: a lured event arrives on a settle, seconds after the harvest, with nothing on screen tying it to the crop — without this the whole feature reads as the Boss button quietly changing colour. It announces once per run and never for an event that was already under way when the game loaded, so a reload mid-event does not reopen it.
- **A ladder is 10 rungs, not 20.** Each rung is two of ZF2's authored HP multipliers added together, so an event carries exactly the HP it always did — 645x `baseHp` — in half as many fights, and a full ladder still pays the same 5,600 gold. The point is the floor: a rung costs at least one attempt however far you overkill it, and the bottom half of the 20-rung curve was a formality for any real army. Merging pairs deletes those without touching the rungs where HP genuinely gates progress. Every prize moved with the ladder, so the first prize zombie sits at rung 5 and every top prize at rung 10.
- **An attempt lasts 60 seconds instead of 30.** Zombies enter the field one at a time, so the window decides how many ever reach the boss at all — 6 of a 20-strong army at 30 s, 13 at 60 s — which makes damage per attempt super-linear in it. A 20-rung ladder cost an ordinary army 135 attempts; that is now roughly a third of it. The whole damage ramp is scaled by 0.8 in the same change, because the second effect of a longer window is that the per-boss difference finally becomes legible (it was worth about one zombie across an entire ladder at 30 s) and the ramp would otherwise have overshot.
- **`baseHp` ramps with the unlock ladder** (±25%, symmetric about General Larvaelus and Mystical Mamba, which keep the source's 2,000). Flat baseHp made the ENTRY event the grindiest, because the ladder was flat while the player was not: a level-24 roster deals about a third less damage than a level-30 one, so Dr. Groundhog cost a moderate army 91 attempts against 63-64 on every boss above him.
- **Boss damage compounds 5% per rung climbed.** Play-tested, a maxed army cleared Loco Locust — the hardest event in the game — in 11 attempts without losing a zombie, taking the top rung in two. HP cannot fix that: HP only buys attempts, so a ladder tuned by HP alone is walkable by any army with enough patience, which is what an endgame event should not be. Rung 1 keeps its authored value, so every event's entry fight is unchanged and only the deep rungs moved (the top of a ten-rung ladder now hits 1.55x). Damage is regressive by design here — it costs a weak roster far more than a developed one — and that is the gate.
- **Activation costs 3-5 brains** on the same ladder, down from a flat 10 (5 for Dr. Groundhog).
- **Brains are a roll now, not a schedule**: 8% per cleared rung, guaranteed on the last one, so a full clear expects 1.72 against a 3-5 brain entry. The old guaranteed 5 meant a player came out ahead simply by finishing, and the event had quietly become one of the better brain sources in the game. Brains are meant to stay scarce, and an event's real payment is its prizes. The guarantee on the final rung exists so the clear that ENDS a ladder always pays something certain.
- A cleared rung also drops a **Brain Ticket** at 1.5% per rung (guaranteed on the last, 1.675 over a full clear) — deliberately a *gold*-side reward, since the event is priced in brains and pays out in prizes.
- **Two decor rolls per clear instead of one.** The 35% rate is the authored one and stays put; it was calibrated against a 40-rung ladder, so it is the roll COUNT that has to move as the ladder shortens. Two rolls at 35% rather than one at 70%, even though the expectation is identical: a single roll can only ever pay one prize, and the second roll re-picks with the first excluded, which is what keeps a 10-rung event able to finish a collection.
- **Boss Token supply quartered** (`SUPPLY_SCALE`), shape untouched — the effective ceiling is 8.75% per harvest. A token buys one attempt, and demand moved twice underneath the old rate: the window doubled and the ladder halved, so a full clear now asks 10-47 attempts while a player was already earning 1.15-3.3 tokens per plot-day. Quartered rather than halved because the attempt count halved; the remaining factor is what keeps an attempt worth a moment's thought.
- **Raid ruleset 27 -> 29** (28 for the window and the ramp scale, 29 for compounding damage): an epic attempt in flight at deploy time settles as `stale_ruleset`. Migrations `0051` and `0052` repair runs mid-flight across the two cuts — `0051` pulls a run parked above the new top down to rung 10 and rewrites its HP columns, since unlike the earlier 20-rung truncation this cut reshaped the curve; `0052` re-fits in-flight runs onto the per-boss `baseHp`. Both cap `current_hp` rather than resetting it, so damage already dealt is kept, and a run that got HARDER keeps its remaining HP rather than having it scaled up — handing back damage a player has already paid attempts for is not a thing a balance change should do mid-event.
- **An epic attempt can no longer be sold to a tab that cannot simulate it.** `/epic-boss/start` now performs the ruleset handshake `/raid/start` has always performed: the client sends its ruleset version and a mismatch is refused with `426 stale_ruleset` before anything is charged, answered by the same reload prompt an invasion uses. The version pinned into a session is the *Worker's*, so the finish handler could only ever detect a stale session, never a stale client — which was harmless while every bump was raid-scene mechanics an epic fight does not touch, and stopped being harmless the moment v28 moved the attempt window. A tab holding pre-deploy JS would otherwise pay its token or brain, fight to a win under the old rules, and be told the boss escaped. Checked before the resume branch too, so a stale client cannot re-enter a live session and hit the same wall at the finish.

### Mutation Almanac
- **A third tab beside My Zombies and the Zombie Almanac, collecting the sixteen mutations.** Same bargain as the species collection: black silhouette until you have owned a zombie wearing it, then the entry opens. What an entry shows is different, because a mutation is not a creature — it is a slot, a stat change and a look that only exists on somebody else's body. So each one is drawn ON a zombie, and specifically on the base Regular of its own tier, which makes the grid run Green → Blue → Red → Silver exactly as the species one does. Tiles carry the name, the stat line and the colour chip; the entry adds the slot it fills, its tier, the lifetime count, and a reminder that one mutation per slot means growing a second replaces the first. Grouped by SLOT rather than tier, because "one per slot" is the rule the whole system turns on and seeing the five ladders side by side is what tells a player which two mutations they are actually choosing between. Undiscovered entries reveal only the crop that grows them.
- **Mutations now carry a tier, 1-4, authored from the source.** ZF2 names it in each market mutant's actor key (`…Tier1Tomatoes` through `…Tier4Heartichoke`) and `MUTATION_CATALOG_CORRECTED.md` tabulates the set; that is what the almanac ranks and colours by. Deliberately *not* the `tier` baked onto a mutant in zombies.json — the Reforged rebalance re-derived that one from unlock level, and it runs 1/3/4 with no tier 2 at all, so a ladder drawn from it would ship with Blue missing. Pumpking is the one mutation with no market mutant to read a tier off (it was always crop-adjacency-only); it is authored as tier 4, beside the two Tier-4 variants, since it grows from the level-39 capstone crop and is the best head in the game.
- **Discovery is recorded by the call that already records the species.** Every path that creates a unit reports it, and a mutation has no creation path of its own, so `recordZombieDiscovered` now takes the mask alongside the key and credits each mutation on it. Saves written before this tab existed backfill from the masks their roster is wearing — independently of the species backfill, since a save from between the two shipping has species counts and no mutation ones. The counts ride the existing `almanac` blob, so online farms carry them with no new server storage.
- **Almanac portraits ignore the "show mutations" display pref.** That toggle is a cosmetic choice about your own army; applied here it would have rendered a catalog *of* mutations as sixteen pictures of a plain zombie. The portrait cache takes an explicit override, and its key is still built from the mask actually drawn, so a forced portrait and a suppressed one can never be served for one another.

### Daily and weekly objectives
- **A daily farm chore is capped at 60.** Measured against what an hour a day actually produces, the top bands asked for essentially the whole farm — 165 harvests against the ~176 a level-44 player with a full field manages in four sessions, i.e. 94% of the day's output for ONE of three slots, at every band. A slot a normal day cannot clear with room to spare is a tax rather than an objective, and since the daily board carries most of the back half's XP it converted straight into slower levelling. The ramp is kept so a level-44 daily still out-asks a level-6 one; it just tops out where a short session can finish it.
- Plowing now shares that ceiling instead of running ahead of harvesting. Once harvest was capped, a plow target of 140 simply became the binding chore and inherited the whole problem — the board still asked for most of the day's field work, through a different slot. The two objectives now largely satisfy each other, which is the price of neither being able to eat the day.
- Weekly harvest gets a **3.3x** multiplier rather than the standard 5x. Its two ceilings disagree with the 5x rule (60 x 5 = 300 against a 200 cap), and capping alone would flatten the ladder — even the lowest band clears 200 — so every band would land on the ceiling and the weekly would stop scaling with level at all.

### Sakura
- New **Sakura Ground** skin (7,500 g), the rung the ground ladder was missing between the 5,000 pair and the moon. Blush-pink terrain strewn with pebbles and grit, hills under blossom to the horizon, and an airy grove ring of cherry over open ground, with rocks, a few stands of bamboo and a pond or two.
- The skin's terrain is the first that is not purely a recolour: `rocky` beds small stones and a fine grit into the ground tiles themselves (`strew_pebbles`), so the valley floor is stony at every zoom rather than only where decor happens to stand. Stone count varies across the five variants on purpose — an even count per tile gives the ground an audible beat at native scale.
- Farm ground and the land around it are now visibly different. Making them seamless was the right first move, but taken all the way it erased the fence line. Both cuts come off the same recoloured tile so they can never drift apart in hue; only the finish differs — the farm carries the stones that come up out of worked ground, and the land beyond is left plain and a shade deeper.
- The land AROUND the farm can now be real ground rather than flat colour (`SurroundingsTheme.groundFill`). Only the farm is built from tiles; everything outside it was the renderer's filler showing through, which nobody notices under a flat terrain and everyone notices under a textured one — the stony Sakura farm was a detailed diamond on a blank sheet of pink. `build_ground_fill` emits a seamless 240x120 rect of the terrain (a true period of the iso lattice), tiled over the land at the farm's own tile scale and phase, so the ground now runs unbroken from the fence to the horizon. Themes without a fill keep the flat colour.
- Scenery pieces can be **mirrored** (`SceneryPiece.flip`), which is what lets a theme built on two pieces of art not read as one stamp repeated: the sakura ring draws both cherries upright and reversed, so half of it leans the other way. Same rule as placed objects — only art with no baked lettering may flip.
- The skin is the first climate that dresses the farm with **weather**: blossom falls over the farm the whole time it is worn. Petals are seeded across what the camera can see rather than across the farm, so their on-screen density is the same at 30x30 and 70x70 and at every zoom, and none of the budget is spent off-camera. Nothing in ZF2 does this — the source has no ambient climate effect of any kind.
- Two new Market decorations, **Sakura Tree** (3,000 g, lvl 12) and **Weeping Sakura** (4,500 g, lvl 18), so the grove can be carried inside the fence. The same two trees are what the skin scatters outside it, and both draw at one authored size (`SAKURA_HEIGHT`, 195px) whether bought or scattered, so a bought cherry stands level with the grove instead of towering over it. That puts them mid-pack among the Market's ornamental trees, which run 166 (Palm) to 225 (Evergreen). Both are the same height; the weeping one is the wider.
- First **contributed art** in the project: both sakura were drawn by **LennyFaze**. Placeables can now carry a `credit`, shown on the card's info parchment — which also means decorations have a magnifier at all for the first time ("About Sakura Tree", where functional items ask "What does it do?"). `tools/contributed_art.py` authors art and catalog row together, on the Memorial Statue's model, so the sprites survive the generator's orphan sweep; sources live in `tools/art/`. ### Art & presentation
- **Autumn has a sunset horizon.** For the hours either side of nightfall (5pm-9pm), an autumn farm shows a hand-drawn dusk backdrop instead of its afternoon one. It is the first backdrop that is NOT derived from the base art by a ramp — a sunset is a gradient sky and warm rim light, which no per-pixel remap of a flat blue afternoon can produce — so it ships whole and carries its own filler and sky band. The window is derived from `NIGHT_START_HOUR` rather than written out, so it can never drift off the moment the lights actually go down.
- A third **Flowering Sakura** (6,000 g, lvl 24), paler than the other two and carrying one open blossom, joins the Market and the Sakura grove. Also by LennyFaze.
- Sakura's wood is arranged rather than strewn. Its two **springs** are placed first, well out from the fence, with a clearing held open around each — a pond is the one thing in a wood you can see across, and the scatter would wedge it between two trees like any other prop. Its **stones** are then laid as meandering trails winding between the trees (`PathSpec`, a random walk with a heading that turns a little each step), not scattered — laid as small stones nearly touching, and started on a stratified grid so they turn up all over the wood instead of clustering where a few uniform draws happened to land. The stone art is a little PILE rather than a single rock, so spacing it out could only ever give a row of heaps — shrinking it to about half and laying it close is what makes a continuous line without turning into a paved gravel strip. The ground is a deeper rose throughout.
- Autumn's haystacks and scarecrows are gone from the scattered props, and Urban's telephone line is sparser. Sakura's wood is now all full-grown trees — no saplings or seedlings — and the only stone in it is the little rocks, on and off the trails alike, so a path is just where they happen to run together rather than a second kind of thing.
- Sakura's sky is warm peach rather than afternoon blue, drawn off the autumn dusk painting's own palette and pitched short of a full sunset — the hills below it are already a strong pink, and taking the sky all the way there leaves nothing in the frame that is not hot. It comes out flat, which is inherent: the ramp maps luminance to colour and the base art's open sky is one uniform value, so a gradient can only be painted, not derived. That is exactly why the dusk piece ships whole.
- Sakura's ground and its hills now read as one landscape. The hills take a faint grain (`TERRAIN_GRAIN`) and the farm's stones were softened well toward the ground colour: flat-shaded hills behind a stony field looked like two different materials, and the stones themselves read as slate chips dropped on blossom.
- **Urban Ground has a street.** A road runs across the land beside the farm with a crossroads and a branch running off it, street lights marching down the kerb nearer the farm (mirrored, so the lamp heads overhang the carriageway), the lot's waste tipped in clumps along the far verge, and a telephone line strung across the skyline under the hills. The scatter can only ever produce a FIELD of things (it walks a lattice and rolls for each point), which is right for a wood and cannot express a line, so a road is a separate pass (`SurroundingsTheme.road`) laid before the scatter, which is then told to keep off the carriageway and both verges. Street lights and barrels have moved out of the random scatter and onto the road: a street light standing on its own in the middle of a lot reads as a mistake, since it exists to light a road.
- **Lunar, Dead, Urban, Sakura and Snowy now have bare horizons.** The original backdrop dots its hills with five broadleaf trees on BROWN trunks, and a trunk is not green — the recolour classifies by green, so no ramp can touch one and every skin inherited five summer trees. That is right for the temperate and autumn horizons (and the beach's distant palms are the point), and wrong for a moon, a desert, a paved lot, a valley in blossom, or a snowfield — on the last of those the trees stayed broadleaf in full leaf while every fir in front of them was under snow. Those five are now derived from a second hand-drawn base with the trees taken off (`NO_TREE_TERRAINS`); it is a build input under `tools/art/`, not a shipped asset. Their `filler` and `sky` moved a shade with the new base and `surroundings.ts` follows.
- **Snowy reads as a frozen wood rather than a play park.** Two thirds of its scattered props were things somebody had built — snow hedging, forts, igloos and carved ice — so a clearing where every third object was a snow fort. Drifts, snowed-under rock and young firs carry it now, with each built piece roughly halved so coming across one is an event.
- **The street lights come on at night.** They cast the same light a Street Light bought from the Market casts — same glow table, radius and reveal strength — so the two are indistinguishable once lit, offset along the lamp's arm so the pool lands on the carriageway rather than on the post. The surroundings' lights are torn down and rebuilt with the ring, unlike a placed object's, which lives and dies with the object.
- **Urban's lot is a dumping ground now, not a pole farm.** Nothing belonging to infrastructure is scattered on it any more: the loose City Lamps went first (there was no honest number of them — the road already carries lamps that light something, so every ornamental one was a second, dimmer kind of lamp lighting nothing), and the stray telephone poles followed, because the line of poles under the hills reads as a line only while it is the only line. In their place the lot is strewn with far more junk, and the junk is rubble: broken stone and dumped crates at two sizes each, with barrels, drums and barriers reduced to a slot apiece and street furniture — benches, postboxes — out of the mix altogether. A lot scattered with benches reads as a park nobody mows. Both rules here are about how often the eye is caught rather than how many pieces there are: one slot in twenty-four sounds rare but the lot draws about a hundred pieces at normal zoom, and a warm brown bench on grey ground is picked out every time. Same reason the Hazard Fence is held to one slot — it is the most obviously urban thing in the library and easily the most saturated, and at any generous weight the lot becomes a field of yellow chevrons. The far band takes the rubble big enough to read from the back, plus the odd bike or crossing sign, so the depth cue survives in a theme with no trees in it.
- **Dead Ground is emptier.** A wasteland at the same fill as a temperate wood is just a wood with dead trees in it — the bare ground between the pieces is the theme — so its scatter drops a quarter. Urban's fly-tipping beside the road is five heaps of three rather than eight of four, which at that spacing had stopped reading as heaps and started reading as a row.
- Star placement tolerates a dithered sky. It matched the modal sky colour exactly, which is fine for flat-shaded art but rejects most of a hand-drawn sky — the no-tree base carries 43 shades of the same blue across one row, all within ±2, which would have thinned the lunar starfield to a fraction of its count.
- Every recoloured backdrop had a teal-green fringe along its distant hills. Those hills are hazed so far toward the sky that blue catches green, and the ramp's "is this a hill?" test requires green to beat blue — so their lit crests (145,210,212) failed it and shipped in the base art's colour on the beach, the moon, autumn and the rest. They recolour with the rest of the terrain now. Cloud edges antialias through the same blue-greens and genuinely overlap them in colour, so the two are told apart by row instead: the backdrop is one fixed authored layout, and its lowest cloud sits 97px above its highest far hill.

### Fixes
- `MAX_FARM_PLOTS` is now derived from the largest farm-size tier instead of the literal 225, which was the 60x60 maximum — a 70x70 farm's last 64 plots were rejected `farm_full` and rolled back.
- A Memorial Statue's occupant is no longer trimmed out of the online bootstrap: the graveyard cap counted enshrined and unenshrined zombies together, so a statue whose occupant died long ago hydrated as a bare plinth once 60 newer losses accumulated (and re-enshrining it was then refused server-side as `statue_occupied`).

### Tooling
- **Four pond rims take hand-measured anchors.** `pond2`, `pond3`, `pond5` and `pond6` now carry measured `ANCHOR_OVERRIDES` entries instead of the anchor `CENTERED_FLAT_TILES` derives for the pond set; `pond1`, `pond4` and `pond7` still take the derived one, so the seven no longer share a single anchor. A measured value outranks a derived one — that is the precedence the generator now applies. **What it costs is on the record**: 0.34 is the fixed point of the mirror `1 - pivotx - 48/w`, the only anchor at which a TURNED piece occupies the same pixels as an unturned one, and the pond set turns (the E and W edges are the N and S pieces mirrored). Off it, a turned piece lands `2*(anchorX - 0.34)*150` px away — pond6 6.3px, pond3 4.0px, pond2 3.3px, pond5 1.2px. So these lay a better unturned pond and a worse turned one. The old "all seven interchangeable, turned or not" test now covers the three pieces that still take the derived anchor, and a second test pins the drift RELATION for all seven, so the trade stays visible while the numbers are tuned rather than quietly disappearing.
- **Tile lab presets.** Drop-in layouts, because what you are judging is how pieces MEET and a seam needs both sides of it: Run, Corner (a bend with a straight on each of its two arms — or all four corners in a row), Crossroads, Closed loop, Pond 3x3, Pond + road, plus the generic Tile out and Surround. The pond layout is classified from the ART — which side of each piece the water sits on — rather than guessed, and the two edges the source never drew are supplied by mirroring the N and S pieces, which in iso swaps the col and row axes and so turns a N rim into a W one. Picking a piece from the palette follows it into its family, so the presets on offer match what you just armed.
- **`tools/tile_lab.html` — a bench for the art that has to meet edge to edge.** Built by `tools/build_tile_lab.py`, double-clickable, art inlined. A flat tile does not sit bottom-centred on its footprint: it hangs off an authored cocos pivot, and those pivots are hand-rounded 2dp numbers that sometimes do not lay a piece where its neighbours are — the apex-south road bend needed a whole tile of correction that no reading of its pivot produces. Until now those corrections were measured by placing roads in the running game and squinting, which is how you end up 3px out.
